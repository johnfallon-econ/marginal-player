The Voters and the Model

In May, 100 voters picked the season’s fifteen best players. The model, which has never watched a game, picked fifteen too. Here is where they split, and what would settle it.

By Ben StupendousOct 5, 20264 min read

In May, a panel of 100 writers and broadcasters picked the fifteen best players of 2025-26. They watched more basketball than I did, and I envy them for it. The model, which has never watched a game, picked its own fifteen. They agreed on twelve.

That’s a lot of agreement for two judges who don’t share a single input. The voters see the games; the model sees the scoreboard while each player is on the floor, and what it costs to replace him. The three places they split are the interesting part, so this column is about those six players, plus the vote they cared about most.

The rules

The model’s fifteen are the season’s top fifteen by Marginal WAR (mWAR), the wins each player added above the kind of player any team can sign for the minimum. It counts the games a player actually played, and since All-NBA dropped positions there’s no quota of guards to argue about.1 Where they split, it goes in the ledger, and I’ll check both sides after next season’s vote.

The MVP

The voters gave the MVP to Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, with 94% of the possible points, and put Nikola Jokić second. The model has them the other way round: 20.1 mWAR for Jokić, 17.6 for Gilgeous-Alexander. The difference is the rating per possession, +7.57 to +6.39, over a similar number of games. MVP voting has never been about one number, and second place on this board is still a long way above third.

The voters’ three

Jaylen Brown made the second team and finished sixth in the MVP vote. The model has him 90th. His rating has sat within a few tenths of average for three seasons running: +0.14 → +0.08 → +0.03 per 100 possessions.2 What the voters see is real: his usage rate, 35%, was the league’s 2nd-highest, and carrying an offence is hard. What the model sees is what happened to the score while he did it. On a max contract, he was worth $8.8M less than he was paid. Those can both be true: a player can carry an offence and still leave the score about where an average player would. Boston traded him to Philadelphia in July. I wanted them to keep him, for reasons the model can’t price; that’s me, not the model.

Jalen Duren and Jalen Johnson made the third team. The model has them 98th and 28th. Here the trend matters more than the level. Johnson’s rating rose each season, −0.29 → +0.71 → +1.32; Duren’s turned positive only this year, −0.41 → −0.42 → +0.30. The model weighs three seasons, so it is slow to believe a jump, and a year of evidence carries an error of about a point either way. The voters saw the jump; the model is waiting for a second year of it.3

The model’s three

Derrick White, Desmond Bane and Amen Thompson didn’t make any team. The model has all three in its fifteen. White made the All-Defensive first team, so the voters noticed him; just not enough. Bane played every game. Thompson, still on his rookie deal, was worth $90M more than he was paid, the most of the three; his twin brother Ausar made an All-Defensive team. These are players whose value shows up in the score more than in the box. One of them is a Celtic, so I’ll leave the arguing to you.

That’s six disagreements for the ledger: three players the voters picked and the model didn’t, three the model picked and the voters didn’t. After next season’s All-NBA vote, I’ll check which picks held up, on both sides.
