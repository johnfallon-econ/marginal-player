The Internet’s Favourite Trades, Priced

We took the most popular trade proposals on Fanspo this month and ran every one through the Trade Lab. Then we asked the only question that matters: who says no?

BSBy Ben StupendousSep 25, 20269 min read

Every day, thousands of people open a trade machine, build a deal that sends their team’s worst contract to a rival for two good players and a pick, and post it with a title like “this actually helps both teams.” I love these people. I am these people. I have built that exact trade at one in the morning and titled it “hear me out.” My group chat has a friend, Mikey, a Knicks fan, who has proposed a trade for every All-Star in the league, several of them twice. He is the target audience for this column, and also its conscience.

Fanspo, one of the most popular of these machines, ranks the proposals by community vote. So we took the 20 highest-rated trades posted there in the month to September 24 and ran every one through the Trade Lab, on the rosters as they stand now.1 9 of them couldn’t be priced: signings and waiver claims, sign-and-trades, and a trade with more teams than the Lab stages. That leaves 11.

How to read what follows. Players is what the players who change hands are worth to each roster, minutes and all. Picks is the draft capital, priced as if unprotected.2 Title odds is the change in each team’s share of championship equity. Net is the sum, in today’s dollars. Every trade has a link that opens it in the Trade Lab, already staged, so you can check the work and then change it until it says what you wanted. And under each, the only question the comment section ever asks: who says no? The team the trade costs more than a million dollars, if any; how often trades graded like it actually left that team behind, because a grade is an average and not a promise; and, on the five I wrote about, whether I agree with the model.

The famous name and the assets

Credit where it’s due: every one of these was a real idea, built and posted by somebody who cares about their team, which is more than you can say for some real deadlines. And the pattern across the month is the pattern across every trade machine ever built: somebody gets the famous name, and somebody else gets “assets.” The model’s view is that the assets are real. In every one of the 8 trades above that moved real draft capital (the † sides aside), the team taking the most of it came out ahead. Build your own in the Trade Lab. It won’t let you win them all. It will tell you why, and then you can argue with it, which is the best part.
