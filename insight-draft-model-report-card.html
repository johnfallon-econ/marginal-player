The Draft Model’s Report Card

15 drafts, graded on the board’s own terms: the stars it saw coming, the ones it didn’t, why its average matters more than its middle, and what it makes of the 2026 class.

By Ben StupendousSep 25, 20268 min read

Let’s start with my miss, since I promised I would. On draft night in 2017 the Celtics traded down and took Jayson Tatum third, and I was furious about it. I had reasons. They were bad reasons. He has returned Boston $911M, the most of anyone in his class. I will ride for him forever, and I will never again trust myself on a draft night.

The board, for the record, had him third. Exactly where he went. It didn’t know either; it just didn’t yell about it.

So this column grades the board the way I should have been graded. The rules first, because they matter more than usual. Before every draft since 2007 the board valued every prospect it could find, using only what was known that spring.1 The value is what the player is worth to the club that drafts him: his rookie deal at open-market prices, then what the club keeps if it re-signs him, which is only anything when he’s good enough to get a max and is worth more than it. Then we wait and see what each player actually returned. 15 classes have played at least five seasons. The board ordered them better than the draft did in ten.2

The average, not the middle

Here is the one idea this whole column rests on. For every prospect the board has two numbers: the middle outcome, what happens if things go about as expected, and the average outcome, which also counts the small chance that he becomes a star. For most prospects those are very different numbers, because a star on a max deal is worth so much more than a bust costs. The board ranks on the average. That is the whole trick, and it’s why the tables below show both.3

And there, at the top, is Nikola Jokić. The board’s middle outcome for him in 2014 was $0.0M: its best single guess was that he would never stick. But its average was $184M, eighth in the class, because it gave a second-round big from Serbia a real chance of becoming something. The league took him 41st, and the pick was announced while the broadcast was showing a Taco Bell commercial.4 He has returned Denver $1678M. If you want the case for ranking on the average, it is a very large Serbian man.

The row that makes me happiest isn’t on the table, because he finished sixth, not fifth: Derrick White, drafted 29th, 13th on the board, 6th in his class. I will ride for Derrick White forever. And in 2014 the board’s number one was Marcus Smart. He went 6th and finished 3rd in a class with two MVPs in it. I have never loved a model more.

These have something in common: their middles were modest and so were their averages. The board saw no path to stardom for Khris Middleton at 38th, and it was wrong. The fair objection: a board built on what happened to players who looked like this will always be late on the ones who become something nobody like them became. That isn’t a bug. It’s what a base rate is.

D'Angelo Russell was the board’s number 3 in 2015, and he has returned his club −$19M. That minus sign is real: the board counts a max contract that pays more than the player is worth as a loss, because it is one. Some of the rest are injuries, which no draft model sees coming, and some are just the board being wrong. It doesn’t get to choose which.

Since we’re being honest: in 2016 the board had Jaylen Brown 6th, and the Celtics took him 3rd. He finished 8th in his class, closer to the board than to the pick. I wanted Boston to keep him anyway. The board and I are still not talking about it.

Notice the shape. The misses on the low side are enormous, because stars on max deals are enormous; the misses on the high side are capped, because the worst a prospect can return is roughly nothing, or a little less on a bad max. That asymmetry is exactly why the average belongs on top of the board, and why a board that ranked on the middle would have had Nikola Jokić nowhere.

Look at the gap between the two value columns. For the board’s top ten, the typical average is 3.5 times the middle. Almost all of what makes these players valuable is the chance, a minority chance, that one of them turns into a star. The board’s number one is Cameron Boozer, who went 3rd, with an average of $868M against a middle of $251M. The first pick, AJ Dybantsa, is 2nd on the board. Ask me again in four years.

Teams watch these players in workouts, meet their families, and know things about their medicals that no model will ever see, so some of these “reaches” are just information the board doesn’t have. The point isn’t that the teams are wrong. It’s that we’ll know, and in four years I’ll open that column with whichever of us looks worse.
