The Draft Model’s Report Card

16 drafts, graded twice: against the teams and against itself. The stars it saw coming, the ones it didn’t (hello, Nikola), and what it makes of the 2026 class.

BSBy Ben StupendousSep 25, 20268 min read

Let’s start with my miss, since I promised I would. On draft night in 2017 the Celtics traded down and took Jayson Tatum third, and I was furious about it. I had reasons. They were bad reasons. He finished first in his class. I will ride for him forever, and I will never again trust myself on a draft night.

The model, for the record, had him 4th. Neither of us had him first. It at least had the decency to be close.

So this column grades the model the way I should have been graded. The rules first. Before every draft since 2007 the model ranked every prospect it could find, college and international, using only what was known that spring.1 Then we wait four seasons and see who was right: the model, or the thirty front offices who actually made the picks. 16 classes have closed. The model ranked the players better than the draft order did in 14 of them.2

A note on the scale: the board scores each player’s first four seasons, quality times availability, on its own ruler. For reference, the median first-round pick since 2007 scored 0.8. Where it helps, the tables also give career wins above replacement, which is the ruler the rest of this site uses.

Some of this is the model doing what models do: it doesn’t care that a player is 22, or undersized, or from a school nobody visits. I’ll admit the row that makes me happiest is a Celtic’s: Derrick White, drafted 29th, ranked 5th by the model, finished 5th. I will ride for Derrick White forever, and now I have a spreadsheet that says I’m allowed to.

And one that isn’t on the table, because he went where he should have: in 2014 the model’s number one prospect was Marcus Smart. He went 6th and finished 3rd in a class with an MVP in it. I have never loved a model more.3

The misses

Now the part the model would rather skip. Two kinds of miss: stars it ranked far too low, and players it loved who didn’t pan out.

At the top of the first list, and at the top of every list of draft misses ever made, is Nikola Jokić. The model ranked him 56th in 2014. Worse, its middle projection for him was the floor: the model’s best guess was that he would never stick in the league. He has 199.9 career wins above replacement. In fairness to the model, the league let him go 41st, and the pick was announced while the broadcast was showing a Taco Bell commercial.4 The fair objection, which Professor Voss would raise before I finished the sentence: the model learned from players who looked like Jokić at nineteen, and almost none of them became Jokić. That isn’t a bug. It’s what a base rate is. It’s also cold comfort.

The other kind of miss is quieter and more expensive, because it’s the kind a team acts on:

These were the model’s top-three prospects who finished well down their class. Brandon Knight was its number 2 in 2011 and finished 48th. Some of these are injuries, which no draft model sees coming, and some are just the model being wrong. It doesn’t get to choose which.

Since we’re being honest: in 2016 the model had Jaylen Brown 11th, and the Celtics took him 3rd. He finished 9th in his class over those four years, which is closer to the model than to the pick, and then he spent the next decade making the model look silly. I wanted Boston to keep him. The model and I are still not talking about it.

The top four are the stars it undersold; the bottom four are the prospects it expected to play who never stuck. Notice the shape. The model’s misses on the low side are enormous, because stars are enormous, and its misses on the high side are capped at the floor, because the worst a prospect can do is not play. That asymmetry is the whole economics of the draft, and I have promised to keep the economics in the footnotes.5

The model’s number one is Cameron Boozer, who went 3rd. The first pick, AJ Dybantsa, is 3rd on its board, which isn’t a knock so much as a coin the model flipped the other way. Ask me again in four years.

The point of the list isn’t that the teams are wrong. It’s that we’ll know, and in four years I’ll open that column with whichever of us looks worse.
