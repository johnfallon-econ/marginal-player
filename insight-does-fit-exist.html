Is a Lineup More Than the Sum of Its Parts?

Do synergies exist, or should teams just roll out the best players?

By Ben StupendousPosted Nov 15, 2026 · Updated Nov 16, 20269 min read

The primary evolution in basketball since the turn of the century has been discovering the value of spacing the floor. In 2004-05, Mike D’Antoni’s Phoenix Suns decided a possession should take seven seconds or less. Steve Nash and three other shooters laid waste to defenses, running pick-and-rolls through wide-open lanes. Threes were 29% of the Suns’ shots, the most in the league; the league as a whole took 20%.

A decade later, the Warriors cemented small ball as a winning strategy, taking 36% of their shots from three.1 Now the average team takes 42% of its shots from three. It is essentially conventional wisdom that a team can’t put many non-shooters on the court at the same time.

Small ball essentially died as bigs got more skilled, and one of the best, Giannis Antetokounmpo, has never been able to shoot. Almost every metric thinks he is incredible. But if you could clone him, would you want five of him, or one and four shooters? Is being a freak enough, or does he need complementary players?

This matters for this site. Every number on this site rates a player on his own, and everything built on them, from the Trade Lab to the GM game, adds those ratings up. If fit is real, some lineups should beat the sum of their parts, some should fall short, and the scoreboard should show it.

Standing on shoulders

It isn’t a new question. In 2013, Allan Maymin, Philip Maymin and Eugene Shen rated every player’s skills, simulated games five players at a time, and found synergies worth as much as six wins between teams.2 In their model, two scorers get in each other’s way (“players must share one ball”). A good ball-handler goes well with an offensive rebounder, and good offensive rebounders go well together.

In 2017, in the Journal of Labor Economics, Peter Arcidiacono, Josh Kinsler and Joseph Price found that players differ in how much they help their teammates produce, and that their pay barely reflects it.3

Both learned from the lineups coaches chose.4

Who picks the lineups

If you just look at how different lineup combinations perform, having more shooting actually looks worse than what mPM would suggest, with lineups underperforming by 0.47 points per 100 possessions for each extra three they are expected to make per 36 minutes.5 These lineups, however, are not chosen at random. Coaches also know that lineups need to do more than just shoot, and so they intentionally mix in players with other skills.6 Late in games, teams that are behind also lean a little more on shooters to try to come back.7 To account for this I use the times when a coach’s hand is forced into particular lineups by injuries.8 When I do that, an extra expected three is worth an extra 0.16 points per 100, which matches the spacing story, but it is not significantly better than just looking at the sum of the lineup’s mPM.9

The spacing story also says the fifth shooter matters less than the first four. When I add a lineup’s shooters one at a time, from best to worst, each of the first four is worth a little more than his rating, between 0.37 and 0.49 points per 100 possessions per expected three, and the fifth is worth 1.06 less. That is what the four-out conventional wisdom would predict, but with this many tests none of the individual steps is significant. If I had to choose between five Giannises and one Giannis with four shooters, I would take the shooters, but not by much.

Everything else

Shooting is only one of the things a lineup can have too much or too little of, so I ran the same test on eight traits (threes, offensive and defensive rebounds, assists, blocks, steals, shots a player creates himself, and usage) and on every pair of traits across teammates, like a good passer next to a good offensive rebounder.10 The two results that hold up best are both about the lineup’s top offensive option.

Heliocentric offenses generally get a bad rap, but our test suggests that having one semi-dominant player can be helpful. For only the player with the highest usage rate, an increase in usage rate is estimated to make a lineup 0.16 points per 100 possessions better than what we would predict from mPM alone.11 A second ball-dominant player may be harmful, as Maymin, Maymin and Shen found that two high-usage players get in each other’s way, which we don’t find, but cannot reject. This aligns with what we have seen from the regular-season success of offenses like the 2017-18 Rockets, where James Harden used 35.3% of possessions and they beat the sum of their player ratings by 5.0 points per 100 possessions.

Shot creation clears the same bar, but it largely measures the same thing as usage. The lineup’s best shot creator is worth about 0.39 points per 100 more than mPM gives him for each extra unassisted basket per 36 minutes. The top creator and the top-usage player are the same person in about half of lineups, and when both are included in the same test, only usage remains.

Rim protection shows a similar pattern to usage, where only one player in the lineup seems to matter. An additional block per 36 minutes from the lineup’s best shot blocker is estimated to make the lineup 0.66 points per 100 possessions better than what we would predict from mPM alone, while blocks from the other four players add nothing we can detect.1213 This aligns with the conventional wisdom that every lineup needs someone who can protect the rim. I think the effect is mostly that you are in trouble if you don’t have any rim protection in the lineup. It could also be a sign that mPM undervalues blocks.

If you have two good defensive rebounders on the floor, they end up taking rebounds from each other. The more each of them rebounds, the less the pair is worth, about 0.42 points per 100 possessions per step, which is what most people would have guessed, although it only barely clears the bar.

Steals are harder to explain. A lineup’s second-best ballhawk is worth an extra 2.71 points per 100 possessions for each steal per 36 minutes beyond his rating, while the third- and fourth-best look like they hurt, although neither of those is significant on its own. My guess is that one player pressuring the ball and one sitting in the passing lanes works, and a third gambler leaves the defense exposed, but I would want to see it hold up for a few more seasons before I bet on it.

What it’s worth

None of these effects are large next to the ratings. If you sort the lineups teams actually play by the sum of their players’ mPM, the 10th percentile is 4.3 points per 100 possessions below average and the 90th is 5.0 above. Moving a lineup’s top-usage player from the 10th to the 90th percentile of usage, 20.5% to 29.6%, is estimated to be worth 1.4 points per 100 possessions. That is about a seventh of the spread in the ratings. The top shot creator and the top shot blocker come out a bit lower, at about 1.2 points each.14

For now the Trade Lab and the GM game will keep adding up player ratings. The usage effect is the biggest one we found, and it is still only about a seventh of the spread in the ratings. If it is still there after next season, I would probably start by docking lineups that don’t have anyone who can carry the offense.
