Can a group of players be better or worse together?

Do synergies exist, or should teams just roll out the best players?

By Ben StupendousPosted Nov 15, 2026 · Updated Nov 26, 20269 min read

Probably the biggest development since 2000 has been an appreciation of the benefits of spreading players around the court. With an aim of no more than about 7 seconds per possession, the 2004-05 Suns sent Nash plus three other good shooters at opposing defenses. They took 29% of their field-goal attempts from three, more than any other team, compared with 20% for all NBA teams.

About 10 years afterwards, the Golden State Warriors showed great success with 4 or even 5 shooters, who took 36% of their field-goal attempts from three.1 All teams now average 42% threes per field-goal attempt. So there seems to be a wide consensus that a team cannot put many non-shooters on the court at the same time.

But as the quality of outside shooting increased among 7-foot players, small ball fell out of fashion, and one of the best players, Giannis Antetokounmpo, has never been able to shoot. By virtually any measure Giannis is outstanding. Would 5 copies of him be ideal though? Or would one Giannis and 4 good outside shooters be preferable? How important are supporting cast to an exceptional talent?

It would be nice to know, given that all players on this site are rated individually, and the Trade Lab, GM game, and other features simply total various combinations of players’ numbers. There should be some lineups which out-perform their constituent players’ totals and others which under-perform.

Standing on shoulders

Others have addressed this previously. Maymin, Maymin and Shen assessed various abilities of all players and ran simulations in increments of 5 players. They conclude there are sizeable positive and negative interactions, equivalent to up to about 6 wins over a season.2 For example 2 players who score lots of points will interfere with one another to some extent; a player good at handling the ball benefits from a teammate good at offensive rebounding, and so do 2 players both good at offensive rebounding.

In 2017, in the Journal of Labor Economics, Peter Arcidiacono, Josh Kinsler and Joseph Price found sizeable variation between players in their ability to raise other players’ output, and little correlation of this with salary.3

Naturally both rely to a large extent on the choice of combinations by coaches.4

Choices of lineups

Naively comparing lineups’ performance according to how much shooting they include, more shooting appears to be detrimental, 0.47 points per 100 for each additional expected three-point goal per 36 minutes.5 But clearly coaches choose lineups with a view to their attributes other than shooting as well.6 And if trailing late, they will tend to select somewhat more shooting to try to catch up.7 So I restrict attention to circumstances forcing the coach’s hand due to unavailable players.8 Then I get a positive effect, 0.16 points per 100 for each additional expected three, reasonable in light of the discussion above, although not statistically distinguishable from zero once the mPM of all players is allowed for.9

Also consistent with this view, I would expect diminishing returns to adding 5th, 6th, and so on good shooters to a lineup. Ranking a lineup’s players by amount of expected three-point shooting, and cumulatively including them, I find all of the top 4 to be worth marginally more than their rating, by 0.37 to 0.49 points per 100 per expected three, and the 5th is worth 1.06 less. However none individually is significantly different from zero given the number tried. So I’d prefer 4 shooters to a 5th Giannis, but only marginally.

Other traits

Obviously there are many other traits where lineups could contain too many or too few good players. So I repeat, first for all eight characteristics, and then allowing for all combinations of pairs of characteristics between different players. The strongest features are both to do with how much one player dominates the offense.10

So, contrary to their reputation, it may be beneficial to have one player dominate the offense to some degree. Including all players’ usage rates, only for the highest there is an estimated benefit of 0.16 points per 100 possessions relative to expectations based purely on mPM alone.11 It might be detrimental, as one would expect, to have two such players (Maymin et al. obtained this result), though the estimate is consistent with zero. Teams such as the 2017-18 Rockets have had very high usage by one player, James Harden using 35.3% of possessions, and outperformed sum of ratings substantially, by 5.0 points per 100.

There is also a benefit to having someone create many of his own shots, 0.39 points per 100 per additional expected successful unassisted shot per 36 by the most prolific. This is likely largely to overlap with the player dominating possession, since it will frequently be the same individual; indeed if both are included only usage is significant.

As with usage, blocks appear beneficial from one player only. The best blocker adds 0.66 points per 100 per expected additional block per 36, and there is no detectable effect of the remaining players’ blocks.1213 This would support the view that it is desirable to have at least one decent protector of the rim. Presumably most of this effect comes from having none, but there may well be an element of under-estimation of value of blocks by mPM.

On the other hand 2 players very good at defensive rebounding would be expected to steal some from each other. Consistent with this, further rebounding by either reduces the value of having both, by 0.42 points per 100 per standard deviation of the pair, although only just significantly.

There seems some benefit of the next best defender against the ball (steals), 2.71 points per 100 per expected steal per 36 over his rating, but negative effects from 3rd and 4th best, neither individually significant. Plausibly it is useful to have 1 pressing the ball and 1 poaching passes, but with 3 others are left vulnerable. But given the lack of significance this may well be spurious.

What it’s worth

All of these are fairly modest in comparison with effects due to differences in quality of players. Considering all lineups actually played, the 10th percentile of sum of players’ ratings is 4.3 points per 100 less than average, the 90th 5.0 more. Top usage rate ranges from 20.5% to 29.6% between these percentiles, so a difference worth around 1.4 points per 100. So it is a fairly substantial fraction, just over a 7th, of the range due to ratings, with the other 2 effects rather smaller, around 1.2.14

So I am not currently adjusting combinations of players for these interactions. If confirmed with data from next season, an obvious change would be to deduct for absence of a player able to assume much of the offense.
