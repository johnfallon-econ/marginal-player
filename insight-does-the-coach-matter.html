Does the Coach Matter?

Coaches get fired for losing. Do they actually change whether a team wins?

By Ben StupendousPosted Nov 15, 202616 min read

Since 1997-98, a head coach has had about a 31% chance of being gone from his club by the next season. When a season goes wrong, he goes first: a lost room, a stale system, a young core that stopped improving. He is the one person on the payroll whose job is to make the players better than they are.

Every number on this site rates the players, and the Trade Lab and the GM game add those ratings up. If coaching is what the firings assume, some coaches should get more out of the same ratings than others, every season, wherever they go. That I can check.

A coach could add value three ways. Scheme: the system he runs, the plays he calls, the adjustments he makes. Development: his players get better than they would have somewhere else. Selection: he decides who plays, and how much.

The data sees each one differently. Scheme is the hardest. A plus-minus rating credits the players on the floor for whatever the system does for them, so a good system gets absorbed into his players’ ratings, and some coaches save their real tinkering for the playoffs. About all the data can ask is whether a coach’s teams keep beating their projections, in the regular season and in May. Development is easier: track how his players improve against everyone else’s around the league. Selection is the easiest, because the ratings say how good each player is, so I can see whether a coach plays the better ones. Even then, he may have reasons the ratings can’t see: fit, a look at a player he isn’t sure about, a young player’s development, or a veteran’s load.

Standing on shoulders

It’s been checked before. In 2009, David Berri, Michael Leeds, Eva Marikova Leeds and Michael Mondello measured how much NBA head coaches changed their players’ production. Some celebrated coaches earned their reputations, and some mostly had good players, but most changed nothing the authors could detect. Their name for the coaches who changed nothing was Adam Smith’s “principal clerks”.1 They measured box-score production. This column uses the scoreboard, which also counts the rotations a coach chose.

The rules

Every test here asks the same two questions of whatever a coach might change. Does it repeat? His odd seasons should look like his even ones. Does it follow him? When he changes clubs, his new team should start to look like his old ones. The second question matters more, because a club that’s good at something stays good at it after its coach leaves, while a coach who’s good at something takes it with him.

Scheme

Start with scheme, where all the data can do is ask whether a coach’s teams keep beating their projections. Take a club’s margin per 100 possessions over a season and subtract what its players’ ratings from before the season said it should be, aging included. What’s left is the season the ratings missed: injuries, luck, fit, and the coach.2

At first it looks like coaching. Under the same coach, a club that beats its ratings one season tends to do it again the next, a correlation of +0.28. When the coach changes, it hardly does: +0.02.3

Part of that is why coaches leave. Clubs change coaches after bad seasons: 62% of changes follow a season below the ratings, against 35% of seasons where the coach stays. A season below the ratings is partly bad luck, and bad luck doesn’t repeat, whoever is coaching. Firing the coach doesn’t help either. Compare a club that changed coaches with clubs that had the same kind of season and kept theirs, and the next season comes out about the same.4

The other side is harder to explain away. When a coach leaves a club that’s been beating its ratings, the club falls back 0.92 points per 100 further than one whose coach stayed after the same kind of season. The drop stays at 0.88 with the share of minutes from returning players held equal, and those clubs had beaten their ratings the season before that as well.5

So does he take it with him? The data can’t say. When a coach changes clubs, his old teams’ record predicts nothing at his new one: −0.14, give or take 0.13. That sounds decisive, and it isn’t. A coach who left a club beating its ratings sees his new club improve 0.25 points more than one who left a club that wasn’t. But the margin of error on that runs from −1.2 to +1.7, wide enough to hold the whole drop or none of it. There are only 95 first seasons at a new club to learn from.6

Part of the problem is the one I started with: the ratings soak up the coach. A player who stays gets rated on last season’s games, so whatever his coach added is now in his rating, and it can’t show up again as the team beating its ratings. A new player arrives with a rating earned somewhere else. So a coach’s edge should carry over more when more of his roster is new, and it does: 0.33 of it into the next season when fewer of the minutes come from returning players, against 0.13 when more do.7 The test can only see a coach through the players who haven’t been credited with him yet.

The standard way to tell a coach from his club is to follow the ones who move.8 Done carefully, it finds nothing: leave out a coach’s whole run at a club at a time, and coaches don’t differ at all. But the coaches who move aren’t a random draw. The bad ones mostly don’t get another job: of the runs in the bottom third, 38% led to another head-coaching job, against about two-thirds of the rest. The best are less likely to have moved too: 57% of the top third, against 76% of the middle. The ones who move are mostly the middle, so following them understates how much coaches differ. Count every coach, and they differ by about a point per 100, roughly three wins a season. The truth is somewhere between that and nothing, and nearer the point than the movers make it look.

That’s the lesson of Gregg Popovich, who won five titles in San Antonio. His teams beat their ratings by 1.61 points per 100 a season, fourth among the runs of eight seasons or more. But none of the Spurs’ coaches since 1998 has coached another club, so the data can’t tell Popovich from the Spurs. To the model they’re the same number. Give coaches their own seat in the plus-minus, next to the players, and he looks even worse: his term is −0.38, 51st of 61, because nineteen years of Tim Duncan soaked up the credit. Over nineteen years, nobody can tell a system from the players who run it.

The case for coaching is strongest in the playoffs: one opponent, up to seven games, time to scheme, and some coaches save their real tinkering for it. If it’s true, some coaches’ teams should beat their ratings in May every year. They don’t: a coach’s playoff runs in odd seasons look nothing like his even ones (+0.08). What carries into May is the regular season. For each point a team beat its ratings over 82 games, it beats them by another 0.38 in the playoffs.9 A coach’s old playoff runs do point the right way at his new club, +0.32, but over 90 runs it’s the same problem as before: too few moves to tell.

Development

Development is more direct. Every player’s season can be set against what was expected of him: his rating going in, aging included, or for a rookie, the draft model’s projection. A coach whose players keep beating those expectations is developing them. It repeats: the correlation between a coach’s odd seasons and his even ones is +0.30. Whether it follows him is the same question as before, with the same problem. At his next club it points the right way (+0.08), but the margin of error runs from −0.12 to +0.28.10 His rookies come closest. A coach whose rookies beat the draft board at his old club tends to see it again at the next (+0.23), just short of beyond luck, and rookies are the one place where coaches still differ once a coach’s whole run is set aside at a time.11 More seasons will settle it.

Selection

Selection is the clearest of the three. Some coaches give their minutes to their best players; some spread them around, trust veterans, or ride a hot hand. Measure how well a coach allocates his minutes: how many more minutes a game a player gets for each point of his rating. A coach who plays worse players ahead of better ones scores low, and one who gives his minutes to his best players scores high. The average coach gives 4.3 more, and coaches differ in a way that repeats: the correlation between a coach’s odd seasons and his even ones is +0.36. It follows him to his next club too, at +0.17, close to beyond luck.12

It pays, too. With the roster’s quality held equal, a coach who gives one more minute a game per point of rating is worth 0.21 points per 100, a margin luck almost never produces. The obvious objection is tanking: a rebuilding club plays its young players on purpose and loses on purpose. The result holds in all four checks for that: contenders only, young players’ minutes held equal, the first half of the season only, and the first half for contenders.

How much? Coaches really differ by somewhere between 0.9 and 1.2 minutes per point: the lower counts only the part that follows a coach from club to club, the higher every coach. A coach one standard deviation better at it is worth 0.5 to 0.7 wins a season.13 Enough to notice, and too little to turn a lottery team into a contender.

Nobody gets fired over it, either. Clubs replace coaches for losing below expectations, for a new general manager, and after a few seasons in the job. With those held equal, how well a coach allocates his minutes doesn’t move his odds at all, and neither does how far his teams beat their ratings.14 (I’d like it noted that Joe Mazzulla allocates his minutes better than all but two of the 30 coaches who started last season. That isn’t why I like him, but it makes me like him more.)

The ice packs

One more thing changes what a team gets from its ratings, outside the three channels: how many games its players miss. That could be the coach, who decides how hard they play, or his medical staff, who decide how fast they come back.

A rotation player missed 9% of his club’s games in 1997-98 and 26% in 2025-26. At first, coaches look like they matter here: a coach’s players miss more or fewer games than their own histories predict, and consistently, with a correlation of +0.38 between his odd seasons and his even ones. That was the era. A coach’s odd and even seasons come from the same years, and the years are what changed. Measure each season against that season’s league average, so the rise in missed games is taken out, and a coach’s players miss games like everyone else’s: the correlation falls to +0.03.15

Then I tried the head trainer, from the clubs’ media guides and Basketball-Reference’s staff listings. A trainer’s players’ games lost do repeat, with a correlation of +0.30 between his odd seasons and his even ones, across 70 trainers. The next trainer at the same club doesn’t inherit it (−0.24), so it belongs to the trainer, and the club keeps none of it. One standard deviation of trainer is about two games a season for every rotation player, about one win, which at 2024-25’s price of a win comes to about $9.2 million a season.16 Strength coaches show nothing.

Only thirteen trainers in the data changed clubs, too few to run the test that matters most: a trainer who keeps players healthy should do it at his next club too. Trainers’ pay isn’t public, but I doubt any club pays one close to $9 million, which is another reason I’d like someone to check.

Repeats: the correlation of a coach’s (or trainer’s) odd seasons with the even ones (*for the team’s season, one season with the next under the same coach). Follows him: whether his record at his old clubs predicts his new club’s, beyond luck (close: nearly; untested: thirteen trainers moved). Strict test: whether coaches still differ with a coach’s whole run at a club left out at a time, his effect and his club’s apart, corrected for noise. Worth, a season, one standard deviation better, where the data can size it.

So the coach matters, and the clearest place is who plays. How well he allocates his minutes repeats, nearly follows him, survives the strictest test, and pays: 0.5 to 0.7 of a win a season for a coach one standard deviation better. His scheme may matter more, up to about three wins, but the ratings soak most of it up and too few coaches move to say. His rookies may develop faster. The rest, as far as the scoreboard can see, belongs to the players.

The GM game now has all of it: a coach with a scheme, a rotation and a hand with rookies, each worth what the data allows, and a trainer who keeps his players on the floor. Hire accordingly. Clubs fire their coaches for losing, and nobody in the data gets fired for minutes.

I’ll rerun this every spring. Watch three things: whether a coach’s rookies keep beating the board at his next club, whether clubs that lose a coach after a good run keep falling back, and the next trainer who changes clubs.
