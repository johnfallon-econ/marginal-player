Does the Coach Matter?

Coaches get fired for losing. Do they actually change whether a team wins?

By Ben StupendousPosted Nov 15, 2026 · Updated Nov 26, 202616 min read

Gregg Popovich guided the Spurs for 29 consecutive seasons, spanning 1996-97 to stepping down mid-2024-25. During this period, the other 29 sides saw 187 different head coaches, while the Kings and the Nets both had sixteen. As noted since 1997-98, there is approximately a 31% probability that a coach will no longer be at their team in the following campaign. And if your team performs badly, the coach is likely to be the casualty.

As explained throughout, each player is assigned a value, and these values are aggregated in the Trade Lab and GM simulator. Should coaches make a difference, then some should be able to generate greater output from identical sets of players, reflected in the final tally.

There are 3 potential avenues for this. First, via their scheme; second, developing the players on their roster; and finally, which players they choose to keep and sign. The first is difficult to assess directly, as some scheme may be implicit in the value of the squad, or a coach may only begin to experiment towards the end of the season. Our only recourse is therefore whether they exceed the projection. For the second, we can examine individual improvement compared to their peers. Finally, for player acquisition, we have a baseline of the players’ expected quality. Of course, there may be other considerations for a coach.

Standing on shoulders

Previous studies have tackled this question too. In 2009, Berri et al. assessed the impact that NBA head-coaches had on their players’ box score output. They concluded that the vast majority of head-coaches made no discernible impact, and likened them to Adam Smith’s ‘principal clerks’. Whilst this analogy may be useful, it seems ill-suited to the body of literature developed around the AKM model in labor economics. Furthermore, we use the scoreboard rather than the box score, which also captures the rotation the head coach chose.1

Scheme

If a club exceeds its projected output under one coach in one season, they are likely to continue to do so in the subsequent season. However, this is considerably reduced when the head coach departs.2

Naturally we must account for coaches departing as a consequence of poor performance. Changes in head-coach are far from exogenous, and in the great majority of instances occur in response to a disappointing season. 62% of seasons that resulted in a change occurred where the team performed worse than expected, versus only 35% where the incumbent remained. Of course, some degree of underperformance is purely stochastic and would not be expected to persist with any successor. Notably, the following year’s output was indistinguishable between teams that changed and those that retained their head coach in these circumstances.3

However, when the coach departed a side that exceeded expectations, we observe that they regressed by an additional 0.92 points per 100 in the following season. My initial worry here was simply that this represented the natural mean reversion of an above-par season, exacerbated by the loss of the coach. But the preceding season was also above expectation for these teams.4

If so, why does this fail to travel with the coach to his new club? Head-coaches departing an over-performing team produce slightly higher-than-average improvements with their new club. The confidence intervals around this are however extremely broad, due to the low frequency of coaches moving, with only 95 observed in their first year with a new club.5

Assuming the squad has adopted their scheme, some of that will remain post-departure. Additionally, some of the coach’s influence will be embedded in the retained players’ value, given that each will have played the previous season under their former boss. A coach’s edge appears to carry over more when more of the roster is new: about a third of the edge carries over when fewer players are back, and much less when more are.6

In the labor economics literature, the analogous approach is to isolate the periods where coaches switch firms. Applying a similar technique (excluding each coach’s full spell at a club sequentially), we find that there is effectively no variation between coaches.7 Of course, we know that the set of switching coaches is non-random. Those who perform in the worst 1/3 of spells are unlikely to be offered a further role, with only 38% progressing to another head coaching job, compared to ~66% of the remainder. Similarly, the best performing coaches are also less likely to leave their current club, with 57% of the top third moving, against 76% of the middle. Hence the population of moving coaches consists largely of median-quality individuals, causing the effect to be biased downwards. Including all coaches yields an estimated differential of ~1 points per 100, translating to ~three victories per season. I suspect that the actual figure lies in-between, and perhaps closer to 1 points per 100.

Consider Popovich, whose 5 rings were won in San Antonio. His side exceeded their mPM by 1.61 points per 100 on average across the 8+ year spells considered, placing him 4th. Since 1998, no Spurs coach has taken another club, making it impossible to distinguish their effect. When allowed to take their “own term” alongside the players, his own term is actually −0.38, 51st of the 61 coaches considered. Presumably a large proportion of this is attributable to Tim Duncan’s presence.

Alternatively, perhaps some coaches only implement significant alterations to their side once the regular season concludes. This would manifest as a consistent pattern of exceeding expectations during the postseason. This is not apparent, nor is there any association between a coach’s performance in alternate years (+0.08). Teams are predicted to exceed their mPM by an additional 0.38 points per 100 in the playoffs, for each point they surpassed it by during the 82-game season.8 Their prior postseason performances are weakly transferred to the new club(+0.32), but given only 90 postseason spells, we cannot draw strong conclusions.

Development

For each player, we assess their performance in a given season vs. their pre-season mPM (aging included), or the expected mPM from their draft profile. We again see some persistence within a coach’s tenure(+0.30), and a small amount carried forward to their next club(+0.08), but the margin of error runs from −0.12 to +0.28.9 A coach who sees his draftees exceed expectations at one club tends to see it again at his next one, although that is just short of significant. Indeed, this is the sole development metric where a coach continues to display heterogeneity in the strictest specification.10

Selection

The most pronounced effect is seen here. We quantify the distribution of playing time across the team by the increase in minutes per game for a unit increase in a player’s mPM. In some cases this will simply involve prioritizing inferior players. Across all coaches, the typical increase is 4.3 minutes per point. This effect persists in the following season(11). To rule out the possibility of this arising from coaches deliberately sacrificing games with their youthful rosters, we repeat the exercise restricting the sample to high-powered clubs, controlling for the minutes of their young players and to the first half of the season.

With the roster’s quality held equal, a coach who gives one more minute a game per point of rating is estimated to be worth 0.21 points per 100 possessions, and that is clearly significant. The result holds in each of these checks.

Accordingly, we find that coaches vary in their allocation of minutes by at least 0.9 and at most 1.2 minutes per point. A coach 1 SD above the mean is thus estimated to yield between 0.5 and 0.7 additional victories.12

Finally, coaches are dismissed for failing to meet expectations, the appointment of a new GM or after several seasons. Controlling for these factors, neither a coach’s allocation of minutes nor the degree to which they exceed expectations influences whether they retain their position.13 (I’d like it noted that Joe Mazzulla allocates his minutes better than all but two of the 30 coaches who started last season. That isn’t why I like him, but it makes me like him more.)

The ice packs

Similarly, the number of games that a side’s players miss will affect their output. This may be driven by the head coach, or alternatively the senior trainers.

A rotation player missed 9% of his club’s games in 1997-98 and 26% in 2025-26. Comparing coaches directly, their players miss a greater or lesser number of games than implied by their historical record(+0.38). However, the bulk of this appears to reflect the calendar, as the odd and even years correspond to the same time periods. Comparing each season to the contemporaneous league average eliminates almost all of this (correlation drops to +0.03).14

In contrast, we find differences between head trainers. There is again some repeatability(+0.30), and over 70 different head trainers. However, the incoming trainer at a club does not appear to benefit from the predecessor’s influence(−0.24). Therefore we attribute the effect to the trainer. An average 1 SD improvement in this metric prevents approximately two games from being missed by each player in the rotation, i.e. one victory. At 2024-25’s price per victory, this translates to a saving of $9.2m p.a.15 No signal can be detected from the strength and conditioning coaches.16

Only thirteen of these staff members moved club, which is insufficient to determine if any positive influence extended to their next club. Nor are we able to ascertain the remuneration of these roles, although I doubt any command anything approaching $9 million.

Overall, the strongest line of evidence points towards the final channel. Assuming a coach is 1 SD superior in distributing playing time, we expect them to deliver 0.5-0.7 extra wins per season. While it is conceivable that their scheme has a larger effect (potentially as high as three), we lack sufficient variation in coach movement to draw firm conclusions.
