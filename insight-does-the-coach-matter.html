Does the Coach Matter?

Coaches get fired for losing. Do they actually change whether a team wins?

By Ben StupendousPosted Nov 15, 2026 · Updated Nov 16, 202615 min read

Since 1997-98, a head coach has had about a 31% chance of being gone from his club by the next season. When a team has a bad year, the coach is usually the first one to go.

Every number on this site rates the players, and the Trade Lab and the GM game add those ratings up. If coaches matter as much as the firings suggest, some coaches should get more out of the same ratings than others, and they should keep doing it when they change teams.

A coach can add value in three ways: scheme, development and selection. Scheme is tough to see because it gets absorbed by player value and some coaches wait until the playoffs before really tinkering. Essentially all we can do is see if a coach consistently beats projections. Development we can see somewhat directly by tracking player progression relative to other players across the league. Selection we can mostly see because we have an estimate of how good players are, but there could be other things a coach is considering like fit, evaluation, development or load management.

Standing on shoulders

In 2009, David Berri, Michael Leeds, Eva Marikova Leeds and Michael Mondello measured how much NBA head coaches changed their players’ box-score production. Some celebrated coaches earned their reputations and others mostly had good players, but most changed nothing the authors could detect. They called those coaches Adam Smith’s “principal clerks”. I don’t think that framework jibes particularly well with the literature in labor economics that follows people who change employers, where who manages you does show up in the results.1 We use the scoreboard and not the box score, and the scoreboard also counts the rotations a coach chose.

Scheme

If you just look at whether a club beats the sum of its players’ ratings, coaching looks like it matters. Under the same coach, a club that beats its ratings one season tends to do it again the next, with a correlation of +0.28. When the coach changes, the correlation is only +0.02.2

Coaching changes, however, are not random. 62% of changes follow a season below the ratings, compared with 35% of seasons where the coach stays. Some of a bad season is just bad luck, and that probably wasn’t going to carry over whoever was coaching. When I compare clubs that changed coaches with clubs that had about the same season and kept theirs, the next season looks pretty much the same, and firing the coach doesn’t really seem to help.3

When a coach leaves a club that has been beating its ratings, though, the club is estimated to fall back 0.92 points per 100 possessions further than a club whose coach stayed after the same kind of season. The drop is still 0.88 with the share of minutes from returning players held equal, and those clubs had also beaten their ratings the season before.4

That drop makes me think the coach takes something with him, but we can’t really see it follow him. A coach who left a club beating its ratings is estimated to see his new club improve 0.25 points per 100 more than one who left a club that wasn’t, but the margin of error goes from −1.2 to +1.7. There just aren’t that many coaches who move, only 95 first seasons at a new club.5

If the team learned the scheme, they can probably still apply it after the coach leaves. Some of the coach also probably ends up in the ratings. A player who stays is rated on last season’s games, and whatever his coach added is already in his rating. A coach’s edge should then carry over more when more of the roster is new, and it does. When fewer of the minutes come from returning players, 0.33 of the edge carries into the next season, compared with 0.13 when more do.6

The standard approach in labor economics is to follow the coaches who move. When we do that carefully, leaving out a coach’s whole run at a club at a time, coaches don’t differ at all.7 The coaches who move, however, are not a random draw. Truly bad coaches don’t get another job, and only 38% of the runs in the bottom third led to another head-coaching job, compared with about two-thirds of the rest. Truly great coaches don’t move as much either, 57% of the top third against 76% of the middle. If the movers are mostly average coaches, following them is going to understate how much coaches differ. If we count every coach, they differ by about a point per 100 possessions, or roughly three wins a season. My guess is that the truth is somewhere between that and nothing, and probably closer to the point.

Gregg Popovich, who won five titles in San Antonio, is a good example. His teams beat their ratings by 1.61 points per 100 a season, fourth among the runs of eight seasons or more. None of the Spurs’ coaches since 1998 has coached another club, and there is no way in the data to separate Popovich from the Spurs. When coaches get their own term in the plus-minus next to the players, his is actually −0.38, 51st of 61. I think nineteen years of Tim Duncan just absorbed the credit.

Some coaches wait until the playoffs before really tinkering, and if that matters, some coaches’ teams should beat their ratings in May every year. We don’t find that. A coach’s playoff runs in odd seasons look nothing like his even ones, with a correlation of +0.08. For each point a team beat its ratings over 82 games, it is estimated to beat them by another 0.38 in the playoffs.8 A coach’s old playoff runs do point the right way at his new club, at +0.32, but with only 90 runs there are too few moves to tell.

Development

We compare each player’s season with what was expected of him, his rating going in with aging included, or the draft model’s projection for a rookie. A coach’s players beating those expectations does repeat, with a correlation of +0.30 between his odd and even seasons. At his next club it points the right way, at +0.08, but the margin of error goes from −0.12 to +0.28.9 Rookies look a little better. A coach whose rookies beat the draft board at his old club tends to see it again at the next, at +0.23, which is just short of significant. Rookies are also the one place where coaches still differ when we leave out a coach’s whole run at a time.10

Selection

Selection is the clearest of the three. We measure how a coach allocates his minutes by how many more minutes a game a player gets for each point of his rating. Sometimes it’s just playing bad players over good players, and a coach who does that scores low. The average coach gives 4.3 more minutes per point. Coaches differ in a way that repeats, with a correlation of +0.36 between a coach’s odd and even seasons. It also follows him to his next club, at +0.17, which is close to beyond luck.11

With the roster’s quality held equal, a coach who gives one more minute a game per point of rating is estimated to be worth 0.21 points per 100 possessions, a margin luck almost never produces. A rebuilding club might play its young players and lose on purpose. The result holds for contenders only, with young players’ minutes held equal, in the first half of the season, and in the first half for contenders.

Coaches really differ by somewhere between 0.9 and 1.2 minutes per point.12 A coach one standard deviation better at allocating his minutes is worth 0.5 to 0.7 wins a season.

Clubs replace coaches for losing below expectations, for a new general manager, and after a few seasons in the job. With those held equal, how well a coach allocates his minutes doesn’t change his odds of keeping the job, and neither does how far his teams beat their ratings.13 (I’d like it noted that Joe Mazzulla allocates his minutes better than all but two of the 30 coaches who started last season. That isn’t why I like him, but it makes me like him more.)

The ice packs

Games missed also change what a team gets from its ratings. That could be the coach, who decides how hard his players play, or the medical staff, who decide how fast they come back.

A rotation player missed 9% of his club’s games in 1997-98 and 26% in 2025-26. If you just compare coaches, their players miss more or fewer games than their own histories predict, and consistently, with a correlation of +0.38 between a coach’s odd and even seasons. A coach’s odd and even seasons come from the same years, though, and most of that is just the era. When we measure each season against that season’s league average, a coach’s players miss games like everyone else’s, and the correlation falls to +0.03.14

A head trainer’s players’ games lost do repeat, with a correlation of +0.30 between his odd and even seasons across 70 trainers.15 The next trainer at the same club doesn’t seem to inherit it (−0.24), and I think the effect probably belongs to the trainer. A trainer one standard deviation better saves about two games a season for every rotation player, or about one win, which at 2024-25’s price of a win is worth about $9.2 million a season.16 Strength coaches don’t show anything.

Only thirteen trainers in the data changed clubs, which isn’t enough to see if a trainer who keeps his players healthy does it at his next club too. We don’t know what trainers get paid, but I would be surprised if any of them make close to $9 million.

Worth, a season, one standard deviation better, where the data can size it.

Of the three channels, the clearest evidence is for selection. A coach one standard deviation better at allocating his minutes is worth 0.5 to 0.7 of a win a season. Scheme could be worth more, maybe up to about three wins, but I don’t think we can pin it down with this few coaches changing jobs.
