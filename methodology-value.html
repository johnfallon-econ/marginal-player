Determining a player’s value

Determining a player’s value involves three stages. First, we must quantify the player’s contribution to their team’s run-rate. This gives us our measure of mPM. Second, we need to convert that run-rate into a measure of wins, which produces mWAR. Third, we must determine the implicit price a team will pay for a given number of additional wins. In each case, we rely on various inputs and assumptions that are fixed, but not learned from the data. These are detailed below, along with some caveats.

As outlined, quantifying a player’s impact requires estimating their effect on their team’s run-rate. There are several decisions to be made in this process, including the degree of pooling of historical data, the regularization applied, and the extent of empirical-Bayesian shrinkage. All are specified explicitly in the code and described below. Of course, each decision will lead to sub-optimal results.

1. Impact: regularized adjusted plus-minus

We begin with the concept of a stint. A stint is defined by the 10 players who comprise a team at any given moment, and so spans from one change to the next. If a given stint s has run-rate ys (per 100 possessions), we can define a regressor xs with 1 if the i-th player is on the home team, -1 if away, and 0 otherwise. We then apply a regularized (ridge) regression:

θ̂ = argminθ Σs ps · (ys − xs′θ)² + λ‖θ‖²

with ps denoting the number of possessions in that stint, estimated from the duration in 24 s per possession. The regularization parameter λ is given by the robust estimate of the residual variance of the OLS fit / 4, implying that the prior variance on each player’s effect is 4 (pts/100)². If there are fewer than 300 total possessions contributed by a player in the relevant pooling period, their variable is combined with other similarly-inactive teammates. Furthermore, the ridge solution is constrained to have a weighted (by possession) mean of 0. Regularization is critical: if many teammates play together frequently, OLS will simply assign one a very large coefficient and the others correspondingly smaller ones. By shrinking towards 0, we encode the reasonable belief that we know little about inactive players.

Multi-season pooling. Given the limited number of possessions in a single season, we extend the pooling to L = 4 seasons surrounding the focus season T. The contribution of a stint in season t is scaled by δ|t−T|, δ = 0.7, such that recent basketball is weighted most heavily, but more distant basketball retains some influence.

There are two variants of this “pooling period”. The “symmetric” period extends L seasons before and after the focus season, i.e. T−L … T+L, and incorporates subsequent seasons. This will give the most reliable assessment of a player’s true value in a given season, suitable for populating the site’s main display of player values. The “backward” period runs from T−L … T, and contains only data generated up to the end of season T. Combining these two periods inappropriately can create an illusion of out-of-sample predictive power.

Standard errors by wild-cluster bootstrap. To obtain standard errors, we perform a wild cluster bootstrap of the residuals, with 400 draws and clustering at the game level. These will be used in the next stage to scale the magnitude of the player’s estimated effect.

Empirical-Bayes shrinkage toward a box-score prior. Finally, each player’s effect is empirically Bayes-shrunk towards a ‘prior’ estimate based on their box-score output. Specifically, we train a gradient boosted model (out-of-fold by season) to predict a player’s pooled stint-based mPM from their average per-100 box stats. Let m̂(boxi) denote the out-of-fold prediction, and Bi the corresponding weight on that:

θ̃i = Bi · m̂(boxi) + (1 − Bi) · θ̂i, Bi = σ̂i² ⁄ (σ̂i² + τ̂²)

Here σ̂i is the (wild cluster) SE and τ̂² is the estimated variance of true mPM around the box-score prior across the player population. As noted, since the former is driven by the multi-season pool, Bi will not vary strongly with a player’s playing time in a given season. Averaged over the published list of player mPMs, we find Bi=0.45 for players under 10 min per game, 0.47 for 10–20, 0.42 for 20–30 and 0.37 for 30+. We adjust the published values each season so that they average to 0, weighted by players’ playing time. The value θ̃i is then the quantity used throughout the rest of the site.

Two priors, not one. It is important to note that the above prior is relatively sparse compared to what is available to us when generating the published (‘label’) mPM. As described, this is estimated using the symmetric pooling period, and so we shrink the stint-based estimate towards a combination of: current box production; 1 and 2-year lags of these; available player tracking data; and a number of ‘teammate-relative’ metrics, including usage, shot mix and % made by zone (rim, mid-range, 3-pt, FT), and assist, rebound, steal and block rates, relative to each of his teammates. A label is allowed to use everything known about a season after the fact. By contrast, to generate the ‘forecast’ mPM for a given season, we pool the backward window’s stints, shrink towards a correspondingly sparse prior (as above, but excluding any lags or tracking), carry the estimate forward for 2.5 years applying an annual drift (a quadratic in age and experience, so varying by player), and re-center. Only its stints stop at T. The prior used to generate this is fit on the symmetric-window stint estimates (the label before shrinkage), with out-of-fold splits spanning multiple future seasons; the variance τ̂² is similarly derived from the symmetric-period residuals. The annual drift is estimated from the year-on-year change in each player’s symmetric-window stint estimate over the entire span of the dataset. Hence, although referred to as the ‘forecast’, it should not strictly be interpreted as an ex-ante prediction at the start of season T. On the site’s own retrodiction test (each player’s prior-season forecast rating times the minutes he actually played, predicting schedule-adjusted team net rating, across 2013-14-2025-26, 390 team-seasons, each rating first given a linear calibration fit on those same team-seasons), the forecast rating misses by an RMSE of 2.79 points per 100 possessions, against 2.99 for the backward-window ridge with no prior, no aging drift and no re-centering. The forecast rating’s design was tuned on that test, so the comparison is in-sample.

A parallel consideration is that the availability of tracking data only commences in the 2013-14 campaign. Accordingly, the label mPM prior differs in this respect pre- and post- that year, meaning there is some degree of inconsistency in the published values. However, the ‘Value Board’ and ‘Trade Lab’ both start from a player’s latest label mPM (all post-2013-14) and project it on an aging curve fit to year-over-year changes in label mPM over every season from 1996-97-present. The ‘forecast’ mPM is instead the metric relied upon for the site’s retrodiction tests and preseason projections.

The above procedure yields a table of mPM values for 13,991 player-seasons, spanning 2,391 distinct individuals between 1996-97 and 2025-26.

2. Wins: a linear probability model

Having determined the number of additional points a player adds to his team, we now wish to translate this into equivalent wins. Conventionally, this would be achieved via some heuristic mapping of points->wins. Instead, we learn this relationship directly. For a given contest g, we compute the aggregate (by playing time) mPM of the relevant set of players:

Xg = Σhome (seci⁄2880)·θ̃i − Σaway (secj⁄2880)·θ̃j
1{home win}g = α + β·Xg + εg

Note that there are 5x2,880s per NBA game. Xg can therefore be thought of as the implied run-rate for the home side. We use the label mPM from the same year (and set inactive players to 0). Estimating on 37,975 contests (including the NBA playoffs):

0.0452β̂ — change in home win probability per additional pt expected run-rate (se 0.00044)
0.586α̂ — baseline probability of home win with equal run-rates
0.178R² — low, unsurprisingly given the inherent randomness of any single NBA game

Although the R² of 0.18 may appear disappointing, we note that each individual game is effectively a Bernoulli trial, and so there is very little information in the dependent variable. Nevertheless, with 37,975 data points, β̂ is roughly 102 standard errors from zero (HC0-robust). Of course, this underestimates the real uncertainty as the published θ̃i are themselves estimated, some using the very same data. However, we can expect these idiosyncrasies to wash out when considering a full season of a player’s activity.

A player playing for sec seconds will therefore add:

mWAR = β · (sec ⁄ 2880) · (θ̃ − θrepl), θrepl = −1.78

to the season’s tally, where θrepl is the published definition of a ‘replacement’ level player. Replacement level is the freely available alternative, not zero, and it is set rather than estimated: a lineup of five replacement-level players on the floor all game is taken to win 8 games of 82. It follows that θrepl = (8⁄82 − ½) ⁄ (5β̂) = −1.78. Our choice of 8 is somewhat arbitrary, though intended to represent a minimally competent NBA team (i.e. one capable of playing 5 bench players). Clearly, altering this will shift all subsequent mWAR. The highest mWAR recorded on the site is 27.7, achieved by Kevin Garnett’s 2003-04. With replacement at 0 the same season would be 22.2.

3. Price: a censored-regression market curve

We are finally in a position to ask: how much is a win worth? Directly observing the salaries paid to players is insufficient: they are censored on both sides, for two reasons. Firstly, the CBA imposes a ceiling on the maximum amount that can be paid to a single player. Consequently, any player accruing >~15 wins per season will receive this upper bound, irrespective of their true value. Secondly, there is a minimum level of payment guaranteed to players, creating a similar floor. Regressing salary against player-season wins will therefore yield a downward-biased estimate of the price of a win. Running this OLS on the subset of player-seasons played >=6,000 seconds in their 4th+ year of service, with salary normalized to that year’s cap, produces a slope of 0.0150 cap share per win. Accounting for the upper and lower bounds in the same dataset increases this to 0.0193. The curve implemented on the site is instead estimated from newly-signed players, using their mean wins (from label mPM) in the preceding 2 years, where the two-sided censored slope is 0.0229; the composite built from that fit and the luxury-tax fit has a linear term of 0.0673 and a curvature term of -0.000405.

Treat both bounds as censoring and estimate the latent curve by full-information maximum likelihood:

pct*i = a + b·wi + c·wi² + σ·εi, εi ~ N(0,1)
observed pcti = min{ max{ pct*i, mini }, maxi }

Note that this means the label mPM has also seen the seasons after the contract in question was signed. Salaries close to or above 25%/30%/35% of the cap (depending on tenure), or at the player’s actual salary where that is higher, are accordingly right-censored. Similarly, salaries <=2.5% of the cap are left-censored. Importantly, we restrict the sample to newly-signed players. Increases to existing players’ contracts are likely to reflect only the provisions of the CBA. A new player’s salary can be identified from the published list as being either: (i) commencing following a hiatus, or (ii) differing by >15% from their previous year’s salary. We consider only those signed in 1999-2025 in their 4th+ year.

Composite curve

As discussed, it is not possible to learn the relevant price curve simply by treating the upper and lower bounds as censoring points. The salient issue is that we lose all information about the price of high-value players. In particular, the teams most keen to acquire these players are prevented from revealing their true willingness-to-pay.

Instead, the published price curve consists of:

  • An intercept, determined by the FIML estimation of the doubly-censored curve described above, (whose own slope is 0.0229). Here, the lower bound is binding and we are able to recover the ‘floor’ for the price of a win.
  • Slope and curvature from a second FIML fit that takes account of the luxury tax. Teams paying above the tax threshold to sign a player s must necessarily be prepared to part with at least s.(1+τ), where τ is their marginal tax rate. We can therefore treat the latter as a (one-sided) censoring point. Of the 2,542 signings, 828 were made by a team over the line; with the 84 maximum contracts signed by teams under it, which stay right-censored at the max, 912 contracts enter this fit as bounds rather than points, and 526 minimum deals as left-censored.

For the slope and curvature, we take the median of 200 draws from a player-clustered bootstrap to ensure that the estimate is not overly dictated by one or more prolific signees. The intercept comes from the single capped fit, and σ̂ from the single quadratic tax-bound fit.

The key condition required is simply that a team is prepared to pay the luxury tax. We place no further restriction on the accuracy of their assessment of a given player’s value.

It comes down to this. From the capped fit, taking out the effects of censoring at the top and bottom and interpreting the resulting line as describing salaries set beneath the cap gives a slope of 0.0229 of the cap per win. Interpreting the new contracts (i.e. those involving Tax Team acquisitions) as providing a lower bound gives a line of 0.0630, or a 2.75× increase, the premium the draft board and the Trade Lab quote. As described above, the “shipped” fit is a quadratic form of this tax-censored line, and hence the coefficient on w is not itself a meaningful measure. At 10 wins however, the marginal value of a season is 0.0592 of the cap per win. All of the valuations presented throughout the remainder of the site, including the conclusions drawn about trades, are derived from this fit. The following discussion of Superstars is a natural extension of these findings. If you are unwilling to accept the tax-team analysis, you can instead use the capped line (which will share the common intercept) and note that the value of a season is divided by 2.35, 2.70, 2.69 and 2.55 at 1, 5, 10 and 20 wins respectively. By definition, surplus will therefore not scale down by these amounts and can be negative.

0.0673b̂ — gradient, i.e. share of cap per win

The value of a season is given by max(â + b̂w + ĉw², 0) scaled by the relevant season’s salary cap. It is floored at zero because a team cannot be paid to hold a roster spot. Negative surplus is perfectly possible and is where the interesting players are.

The curvature term is not statistically distinguishable from zero. ĉ is the median of the bootstrap draws, -0.000405; 34% of the draws are positive, and their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles are [-0.00197, 0.00274], straddling zero. Note that the CI does not center around ĉ (the bootstrap median), because the draws are skewed: the positive draws run further from zero than the negative ones. The median of the bootstraps implies a weakly-concave function, whereby each successive win is worth marginally less than its predecessor. This leads to a shortfall of 16.2% of the cap at 20 wins relative to a simple linear fit with the same gradient. Of course the true parameter may well be flat, and the alternative presentation is equally consistent with our findings.

Why the Value Board outstrips the biggest contract

The picture is now complete and yields an almost unbelievable conclusion. As noted earlier, a 20-win season has an implied value of ~119% of the salary cap, but the maximum existing contract represents just 35% of the cap. We thus expect individual Superstars to command a value greater than the salary cap, which is distributed across a whole squad. This is driven by the linear term.

There is clearly a massive redistribution of wealth from the elite Superstars to their clubs, as dictated by the CBA, and most heavily concentrated at the top end of the talent distribution. The first entry of the Value Board seeks to quantify this.

Different Value Boards

As you will see from the site, there are 2 Value Boards.

  • Players - comprise the set of individual season boards covering the period 1999-00-2025-26. These represent known quantities: i.e. how much each player contributed (as measured by mWAR) and how many minutes they played, in order to infer their market value ex-post.
  • Value Board - the current board. For the ongoing season, this is primarily prospective: it takes each player’s most recent mPM and rolls them forwards on the aging curve, before applying the market fit to obtain a market value. This is displayed alongside their contemporaneous mWAR (an ex-post quantity). Alternatively, selecting a past season will return the ex-post market value of each player (scaled by their mWAR), as in the Players’ boards, to which the remainder of this section pertains.

Caveats on the ex-post boards

Firstly, they do not indicate what the Value Board would have predicted for those seasons. Nor can we claim that any particular player would have been discernible from the available information. To properly assess this, we would need to refit the aging curve, price curve and wins-scaling using the data observable at each point in time.

Secondly, seasons near either end of the panel are less retrospective than the rest. Each player’s mPM is an average of their performance in the T−4 … T+4 seasons surrounding the target season. For the final 4 seasons, the latter leg of this window is truncated, and in the case of the most recent season, completely. Similarly for the initial 4 seasons. However, each player’s minutes played will be fully known, so the only limitation to the accuracy of the mPM will be the amount of data contributing to its calculation. Comparing the most recent season (2025-26) to the set of seasons between 2000-01 and 2021-22, where the full window is available, we see that its mPM is ~16% more uncertain, and pulled ~18% closer to the box-score baseline (0.49 vs 0.42). The effect is smaller for 2024-25 (0.46) and the earliest season in the dataset (1996-97, 0.48).

Accordingly, the most recent season(s)’ mPM should be treated with caution, and will be updated as subsequent data becomes available. In addition, we should expect some movement of the other seasons’ values in future iterations, because the box prior, τ̂² and the per-season centering are refit on the whole panel each time. This is a feature of the method, not a bug.

Limitations

  • One price curve for two decades. Thirdly, a single market and wins-fit is imposed across the full length of the dataset. Only the magnitude of the salary cap differs from season-to-season. This presumes a certain degree of similarity in the NBA’s labor market over this period, which is left untested.
  • Plus-minus cannot separate perfectly collinear teammates. Fourthly, it is impossible to identify the distinct effects of co-linear players. While the regularization process will mitigate this to some extent, two players who never appear apart are not identified, and no regularization creates information that is absent.
  • Salary data has holes. Fifthly, there are missing salary values. Between 84% and 100% of players have a known salary on the Per Season boards. Only in the seasons 2013-14 (84%), 2022-23 (91%), 2023-24 (90%), 2024-25 (89%) and 2025-26 (89%) does this fall below 93%. Where salaries are not recorded, the associated surplus is marked as ‘Unknown’. It should be noted that this is distinct from the (implicit) zero surplus.
  • Value is not the same as contribution to winning a title. Lastly, although the number of wins generated by each player can be determined, the board tells us nothing about the likelihood of them helping their side secure a championship.
