The Best and Worst Trades, Graded Twice

Every NBA trade since 1999-00, graded once on what was knowable the day it was made and once on how it turned out.

By Ben StupendousPosted Oct 5, 2026 · Updated Nov 26, 20265 min read

When OKC were unable to reach a contract extension with Harden in Oct 2012, they sent him to the Rockets shortly before the start of the campaign. Running our Trade Lab through with just the relevant data from that date yields a grade of +$210M for Houston. In fact, +$945M, i.e. four times more, and the second-best result of any trade old enough to grade.

As soon as a significant trade occurs, the public will pass judgement, often almost immediately. For many years, Kevin Pelton has published letter-grades of major transactions, while supporters will continue to revisit and assess these years down the line. This is known in the gambling world as “resulting” - evaluating your play by its eventual outcome.1

The rules

I have assessed each trade on two criteria: process, i.e. the value of each side according to the Trade Lab on the transaction date, and result, i.e. the value actually returned to that side (converted to current$).2

The sides the model liked on the day lost value one time in seven and the ones it disliked came out ahead one time in four; ranking the trades by the process grade, the best fifth returned +$100M a side on average against −$102M for the worst fifth.3

The best process on record is my team’s. Back in July 2007, Boston parted with 5 players and 2 #1 picks to acquire Kevin Garnett, a transaction which received a process-grade of +$949M, and delivered +$761M plus a championship.4 The worst on the day is Chicago’s 2017 trade of Jimmy Butler to Minnesota, graded −$715M. By contrast, among the worst on both metrics is Boston’s acquisition of Kyrie Irving ten years later, sending Isaiah Thomas, Jae Crowder, Ante Zizic and Brooklyn’s #1 pick. I can still recall the glee I felt at the time... (Sigh).

Oklahoma City sent Paul George to the Clippers in exchange for Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, Danilo Gallinari and multiple 1st-rounders. This has returned the highest value of any trade, +$1.5B. At the time of the trade, the model also assessed the transaction favorably, despite placing George (then a star) higher than Gilgeous-Alexander, who had one season behind him. The lowest return is New York’s 2004 trade for Stephon Marbury from Phoenix: −$755M, against −$493M on the day.

Results count only trades at least four seasons old, so most of the contracts involved have run their course: 2,042 team-sides. Sign-and-trades are left out, 125 of them: the player picked his club, and the trade was paperwork. The remainder is detailed in Methodology. ↩
  3. Across all 2,471 team-sides, the correlation between the two assessments is 0.64. Naturally, a large degree of this is driven by the common input required to calculate the process-grade, namely the valuation of the outgoing assets. ↩
