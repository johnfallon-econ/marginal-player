The Best and Worst Trades, Graded Twice

Every NBA trade since 1999-00, judged once on what was knowable the day it was made and once on how it turned out. They are not the same list, and the gap between them is the whole story.

By Ben StupendousSep 25, 20266 min read

Poker players have a word for judging a decision by how it turned out: resulting. It is the most common mistake in sports analysis, and the most fun. The team that traded for the guy who got hurt made a bad trade. The team that drafted the guy who became a star made a great pick. Case closed, next caller.

The trades page grades every NBA trade since 1999-00 two ways, and this post is about the difference. Process is what the Trade Lab would have said on the day, using only what was known then: each player’s Marginal Plus Minus (mPM), age and contract, and the picks, as they stood. Result is what the trade actually did for the club: what the players and picks it got actually returned, less what the ones it sent were projected, on the day, to give it had they stayed. That second half matters: a club is charged for what it sent away at the forecast, so a player who blossomed somewhere else costs it nothing here. Both grades are the change in each team’s roster value, in today’s money.1

Does a good process buy a good result? Mostly. Of the 2,294 team-sides old enough to judge, one the model liked on the day still lost value one time in six, and one it disliked came out ahead one time in four.2 Remember the number. It comes back.

The best process

The single best trade the model has ever graded on the day it was made is one nobody thinks of as a trade at all: the 2010 sign-and-trade that sent LeBron James to Miami. The Heat’s side grades at +$1.9B; Cleveland’s is the worst process on record, at −$2.4B. In fairness to Cleveland, its alternative was getting nothing at all, and this is not the kind of decision the Lab can see.3

The best result

Here is the headline. The best result of any trade on the board is Oklahoma City’s side of the 2019 deal that sent Paul George to the Clippers and Shai Gilgeous-Alexander the other way, along with a stack of picks: +$1.5B. On the day, the model graded Oklahoma City’s side at −$40M, which is to say roughly even. The Clippers’ side graded at −$12M and has returned −$413M.

That is not the model being wrong. It was a fair trade the day it was made. A guard coming off a good rookie season was worth about what the model said he was worth, and nobody, including the Thunder, knew he would become an MVP. The model gives Oklahoma City no credit for the process, and I’d give it a little: the picks, the patience, the bet. Nobody gets credit for knowing. Nobody knew. That’s what makes it a great story instead of a great decision.

Good process, bad luck

The sides the model liked on the day that went on to lose the most. The reason is always on the receiving end: what a club got came in well under its forecast. What it gave away is charged at the forecast, however he turned out. Top of the list is Brooklyn’s side of the trade for Kevin Garnett and Paul Pierce, the rare deal in which Brooklyn was not the team holding the picks.

Bad process, good luck

Now the mirror image: sides the model graded as losers on the day that came out furthest ahead. Some of them were smart in ways the model couldn’t see, and some were lucky. The model can’t tell those apart, and, if we’re being honest, neither can any of us. On the list, I’m obliged to disclose, is Boston’s side of that same Brooklyn trade: graded a loss on the day, it brought back the Nets picks that became Jaylen Brown and, after a swap, Jayson Tatum. (I’ll take it.)

What to take from it

The grade on the day is a good guide: get the process right and the trade usually comes out ahead. Usually is not always. One in six still goes wrong, so a front office that makes a few dozen good trades will watch a handful blow up anyway, and one that makes bad ones will get away with a few. Judge the process. It’s the only part anyone controls. Then check back in five years anyway, because it is extremely fun.
