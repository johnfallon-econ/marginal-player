The Most Valuable Role Players

Why the league’s most impactful contracts aren’t always attached to the stars.

BSBy Ben StupendousSep 25, 20264 min read

Every good team has one. The guy who plays 24 minutes, never has a play called for him, and somehow the team is better every time he checks in. Broadcasters call him a “glue guy,” which is a compliment in roughly the way “he has a great personality” is a compliment. Every fan has a favourite one. Mine is Marcus Smart, and it will always be Marcus Smart, a man who took charges as if each one were a personal matter between him and the other guy.

The model has no idea what glue is. It has never watched a game, never seen a charge, never heard a broadcaster say “intangibles.” My friend Deb, who has had Celtics season tickets since the Pitino years, considers this disqualifying, and on some nights I agree with her. But it counts. It just counts. And by its count, some of the best value in the league in 2025-26 came from exactly these players: the ones nobody puts on a billboard.

The ground rules

A role player here is anyone who averaged under 28 minutes a night and earned under 10% of the cap ($15M).1 319 players qualified. Together they earned $1411M and produced $2813M of surplus. That’s more surplus than all 37 max contracts in the league combined ($1025M).

Julian Champagnie leads: 6.2 wins above replacement in 27.9 minutes a night, for $3.0M. Moussa Diabaté is next, at 6.0 wins for $2.3M. For context, 6.2 wins is what the model credited to plenty of players earning ten times as much.

Now look at the salary column. Five of the top ten earned less than $4.6M, about 3% of the cap. That is, give or take, what it costs to employ someone the league considers interchangeable. They were not interchangeable.

Why the market keeps missing these guys

The fair objection first, because it’s a good one: maybe the market isn’t missing anything, and these are small samples in small minutes that will regress the moment somebody pays them. Some of them will. That’s what the footnote at the bottom of this section is for.

Some of this is the rulebook again: six of the top ten are on rookie-scale deals, whose price the collective bargaining agreement sets before anybody has seen them play. Those aren’t mistakes so much as the league’s entry fee paying off.

The rest are the interesting ones. One plausible story, and it is only a story: salaries follow what a player does with the ball, and ratings like this one follow what happens to the scoreboard while he’s on the floor. Those are different things. A screen that frees a shooter, a rotation that kills a drive, a big man who is simply always in the right spot; none of it shows up in a box score, all of it shows up in the margin, and only one of those two gets priced in July.2

A last note on what success looks like for a role player. Payton Pritchard would have topped this list, except he played 32.3 minutes a night and graduated out of the category. That is what happens to role players who are this good: they stop being role players. Their salaries, eventually, notice.

Figures: the 2025-26 season on the retrospective players board, as of this build. Wins are on this model’s own scale and dollars at the open-market price of a win; the method explains both.
