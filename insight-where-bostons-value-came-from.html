Where Boston’s Value Came From

The Celtics finished 4th in the league in total roster surplus with their best player out for most of the year. The credit goes to the rest of the roster.

BSBy Ben StupendousSep 25, 20264 min read

Full disclosure, since you’d figure it out by the second paragraph anyway: I grew up in Boston, I sat through the Antoine Walker years, and I have wanted the Celtics to win every game I have ever watched. (I also hated the Tatum pick on draft night. I have since recovered, and I will ride for him forever.) That’s why this column leans on the model harder than usual. Here is the Celtics’ 2025-26 season in two sentences. Their two max-contract stars, Jayson Tatum and Jaylen Brown, combined for −$36M of surplus. That is a minus sign, and you are reading it correctly. The team as a whole finished 4th in the league in total surplus, at $327M.

That shouldn’t be possible. (Checking.) It is. It’s a little like a restaurant getting its best review in the year the chef was out.1

The four guys

Derrick White, Payton Pritchard, Neemias Queta and Sam Hauser. Combined salary: $48M. Combined surplus: +$256M, or 78% of Boston’s total. All four rank in the league’s top ten bargains among negotiated contracts, which is a sentence no other team can say about even two of its players.2

Derrick White is the headliner, and a man I will ride for forever: 10.3 wins above replacement on $28M, which is 18% of the cap. Those are star wins at a starter’s price. But the line that should be framed in the Celtics’ front office is Neemias Queta’s: $2.4M in salary, 5.7 wins, production the market prices at $59M. If you have ever wondered what a market inefficiency looks like in the wild, it looks like a backup center playing 25.1 minutes a night for about the price of a nice house in a Boston suburb.3

And the stars?

Jaylen Brown played 71 games and rated +0.03 per 100: about league average, on a contract priced for a star. That’s the whole of his −$8.8M. In July, Boston traded him to Philadelphia for Paul George and a stack of picks. That number is the case for the trade. I argued against it anyway, loudly, in the group chat, and I would again. Some of that is being from Boston. But not all of it. How a team treats the players who stay is a reputation, and reputations last: they shape who wants to sign with you, and, I think more importantly, how much the players you already have grow while they’re with you. The model prices what Brown produced. It has no column for what trading him says to everyone else in the building.

Econ Corner: the gift exchange (Skippable. I know.) Economists have a name for this: the efficiency wage, and in particular George Akerlof’s idea of labor contracts as a partial gift exchange. A firm pays, or keeps, a worker on better terms than the market strictly requires; the workforce reads that as a gift and answers with effort and loyalty the firm could never write into a contract. Loyalty to a long-tenured star is that kind of gift, and it is paid to the whole roster, not just to him. Professor Voss would add, correctly, that the field evidence is mixed: in experiments the effort bump from a gift often fades within hours. A locker room isn’t a lab, and a decade isn’t a few hours. But I can’t put a number on it, which is exactly why the model can’t either.

The model’s contribution is narrower, and possibly more useful. Boston has made its call on one of the players it paid the most. The ones it truly can’t afford to lose are the ones it pays the least. Those four produced 78% of the team’s surplus. Surplus is what pays for everything else.
