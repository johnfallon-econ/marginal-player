Is Anybody Actually Good at This?

Every trade since 1999 and every draft pick since 2007, sorted by who made it. I can’t tell any front office from a lucky one. I can tell you what happens when you hand one the model.

By Ben StupendousSep 27, 202611 min read

In Boston we called him Trader Danny, and about half the time we meant it as a compliment. Danny Ainge would trade anyone, for anything, at any hour, and for most of two decades I was sure it made the Celtics smarter than everybody else. I am from Boston. I would like that to be true. This column is me finding out whether it is, and it turned into two questions.

The first: do front offices differ from each other? Not who made the best trade or drafted the best player. Somebody always did. Whether knowing who made a decision tells you anything about how it grades, once you account for the year it was made in, and whether that difference is clear enough that it isn’t just the dice.1 The second: do they beat the model? What would each club have done if it had simply followed it?

Two things to be good at. Process is making the decision the numbers liked on the day: for a trade, the Trade Lab’s grade; for a pick, what the draft board thought the player was worth to the club that took him, against a typical pick in that slot. Beating the process is what actually happened, minus what decisions graded like yours usually produce. That second one is where scouting, development and luck all live, and it’s where a front office would show up if it saw things the model doesn’t. And two ways to sort the decisions: by franchise, and by the person running it on the day.2

Econ Corner: what “consistently” means (Skippable. I know.) Give every front office its own average, after taking out the year. If front offices really differ, those averages spread out more than they would if you dealt the same decisions to teams at random. So deal them at random a couple of thousand times and count how often random dealing spreads them as far. That’s the test. The size is the spread left over once you subtract what random dealing produces on its own; below I call it the true spread. And because I’m looking at 30 teams at once, one of them will always look special; the fix for that is a false discovery rate, which asks how often you’d be wrong calling a club this far out real, given how many I looked at.

Do they differ? I can’t tell

2,763 trade sides since 1999-00. One side’s grade swings by about $98M either way, in today’s money. The true spread between franchises, once the dice are out, is $3.8M a trade, and random dealing spreads the teams this far 28% of the time. By executive there is no spread left at all.

Now the part I was dreading. Boston is second of 30, at $21M a side above the league, and on its own that clears the usual bar for “not luck”. So does Miami. But with 30 teams one or two will clear that bar by luck, and once you allow for having looked at all 30, the best any club manages is this: call it real, and you should expect to be wrong about half the time.3 So I can’t tell Trader Danny from a lucky coin. (Sigh.) I would like the record to show that the coin came up heads a lot.

Count the asterisks: seven, across four panels of 30, where luck alone would hand out about six. Miami and Boston on trade grades, Utah beyond them, Dallas and Houston on the picks themselves, and, bottom right, beyond the board, Oklahoma City and Denver.

That is not the same as nobody being better. It means that a club would have to be enormously better to show up. To be picked out reliably from 30, a club’s edge would need to be about $40M a trade, roughly 10 times the true spread, or $38M a pick. A real edge of a few million a decision, which is a lot of money over a decade, is invisible in 27 seasons of trades and 20 drafts.4

The draft comes closest. By franchise, the board’s value of each pick against a typical pick in that slot spreads out further than random dealing manages 94% of the time: short of the usual 95%, but closer than anything in trades. And one version repeats itself: how close each first-round pick was to the board’s favourite of the players still available. A franchise’s average in odd-year drafts predicts its average in even-year ones, at a correlation of 0.55.5 Miami and the Lakers take the board’s favourite most often; Washington and Utah least. The caveat: “good process” here means agreeing with my model, which might be good drafting or might just be liking the same kind of player it does.

Beyond the board is where front offices would show up if they saw things the model doesn’t, and it is the one place they differ as a group: by executive, under 1% by the usual test, 2% by the reshuffle, with a true spread of $23M a pick. I just can’t tell you who. Allowing for having looked at 22 executives, the closest to a name is Sam Presti, at a false discovery rate of 16%.

Take out Nikola Jokić and the result still clears. Take out Nikola Jokić, Damian Lillard and Russell Westbrook and it doesn’t (11%); take out all ten and it’s nowhere (51%).6 Most of these executives did it once, and one draw can’t tell an eye for stars from standing in the right place.7 The exception is Sam Presti, with three of the ten: Kevin Durant, Russell Westbrook and James Harden, in three straight drafts. And sixth on the list is Jayson Tatum, the pick I was furious about in 2017. The board had him third, where he went. Nobody had him that good, least of all me.

Do they beat the model? The model beats them

The first question compares front offices with each other. This one compares them with the model, and the answer is different. Take one club at a time and let it draft off the board: at every pick, the board’s favourite of the players still available, while every other club drafts exactly as it did.8 Score each pick on what the player actually produced in his first four seasons, the rookie deal, at what those wins are worth on the open market, wherever he played. Salary cancels: the board’s pick signs at the same slot.

The board would have changed 84% of 900 picks since 2007. Every club but Miami comes out ahead, by +$33M a pick on average: +$48M in the first round, where the board’s player was the better one in 64% of the picks it changed, and +$18M in the second. It isn’t a few stars. Take out every swap that lands on Jokić, or the twenty biggest gains, and it barely moves.9

The board would have taken Stephen Curry for Minnesota instead of Jonny Flynn. It would also have passed on Damian Lillard, and on Paul George, Khris Middleton and Desmond Bane. It misses stars. It just misses fewer of them than the people doing it for a living, and it never falls in love with a workout.10

Trades, the same way. Let the model veto every trade it graded negative for one club, and let that club simply not make it.11 The model is a grump: it would have vetoed 59% of 2,177 trade sides. But the ones it hated went badly, −$13M on average against +$23M for the ones it liked, and 24 of 30 clubs come out ahead. The fair comparison is vetoing the same number of a club’s trades at random, which isolates the grade from simply trading less: the model does better for 26 clubs, and clearly better, allowing for having looked at 30, for Brooklyn, Charlotte, Philadelphia and Sacramento.

And then there is my team. Utah and Boston lose the most from the veto: Boston’s trades the model hated worked anyway, and the veto costs it $684M. Vetoing the same number of Boston’s trades at random would have cost $1230M, so the model still picked out Ainge’s weaker ones. They just weren’t weak enough. The costliest vetoes anywhere are the stuff of legend: Oklahoma City’s trade of Paul George that brought back Shai Gilgeous-Alexander (+$1911M), Toronto’s for Kyle Lowry (+$1032M), and Utah’s draft-night deal for Rudy Gobert (+$930M).

Next time somebody calls a GM a genius, ask how many Jokićs that is, and whether the board would have taken him first. Then go grade a few trades yourself on the trades page. It’s more fun than being right.
