Is Anybody Actually Good at This?

Every trade since 1999 and every draft pick since 2007, sorted by who made it. I can’t tell any front office from a lucky one. I can tell you what happens when you hand one the model.

By Ben StupendousSep 27, 202620 min read

Fire GarPax. Joe Dumars on two phones. Isiah Thomas and the Knicks. Every fan knows a front office that was dumb. It isn’t a debate; it’s something we were raised on.

I was raised on the other side of it. Growing up in Massachusetts, I believed the public schools and the universities made everything here run a little smarter, and that the Celtics hung all those banners because Red Auerbach knew things nobody else knew. Like every fan, I also assumed that given the phone, I’d know better. Somewhere along the way, arguing about transactions became a bigger part of being a fan than watching the games.

Then there’s the Baltimore stockbroker. He mails 10,240 strangers a stock tip: half are told it goes up, half that it goes down. Next week he writes only to the 5,120 who got the right call, and splits them again. After ten weeks, ten people have watched him call the market ten times in a row without a miss. They’d hand him their savings. He never knew anything.

Thirty front offices have been making calls for decades. Some of them are going to look like geniuses.

I built a draft model and a trade model because I was bored. The draft model liked Jokić more than anyone: 4th in his class, when the league took him 41st. It liked Shai 3rd in 2018, when he went 11th. It still had no idea they should have gone first by a mile. In fairness, neither did the GMs we call geniuses. Which raises two questions: are our heroes just our villains with better luck, and could you use a model like mine to beat them? I’ll take the second one first, because if the model can’t beat anyone, you shouldn’t trust it to judge them.

Could the model win? Yes

Before you trust any of this, a confession: I know how these careers turned out, and so does anything I build today. The fix is to make the model take the test the GMs took. Every draft on the board was predicted by a version of the model trained only on the drafts before it: 2009 never saw Steph Curry’s career, and 2014 never saw Jokić’s. Statisticians call these out-of-fold, or walk-forward, predictions.1 The trade grades work the same way: each player is rated on what was known the day of the trade. Two things I can’t scrub. The scouting reports were scored by an AI model trained after these careers played out; it sees only the report, never the name, but a report can be recognisable. I also chose how to build the model, knowing who turned into who. So treat the gap below as an upper bound on what a club could have had.

Flip one pick at a time. Everything before it happens exactly as it did; at that pick, the club takes the board’s favourite of the players still available instead. Then add up the gaps, club by club.2 Score each pick on what the player was worth to the club that drafted him over his career, by the board’s own rules: the rookie deal at market value, and after it only what a max deal leaves on the table, since a player on any other deal is paid what he’s worth.3

The board would have changed 84% of 1,133 picks since 2007. Every club but Indiana and Miami comes out ahead, by +$61M a pick on average. Indiana and Miami beat what the board would have taken, though not by much: $4.0M a pick over 39 and $2.5M a pick over 22. I can’t say the board beat everyone. Across the league it gains +$83M a pick in the first round, where the board’s player was the better one in 61% of the picks it changed, and +$40M in the second. A lot of that is one man. The board takes Jokić at 18 different picks in 2014, and those swaps alone are worth about half of it: per changed pick, the gain is +$73M with them and +$36M without. The typical first-round swap gains +$20M. Still a gain.4

The board would have taken Nikola Jokić for Toronto instead of DeAndre Daniels. It would also have passed on Damian Lillard, and on Donovan Mitchell, Paul George and Bam Adebayo. It misses stars. It just misses fewer of them than the people doing it for a living, and it never falls in love with a workout.

Trades, the same way. Let the model veto every trade it graded negative for one club, and let that club simply not make it.5 The model is a grump: it would have vetoed 59% of 1,948 trade sides. But the ones it hated went badly, −$13M on average against +$21M for the ones it liked, and 21 of 30 clubs come out ahead. The fair comparison is vetoing the same number of a club’s trades at random, which isolates the grade from simply trading less: the model does better for 25 clubs, and clearly better, allowing for having looked at 30, for Brooklyn, Charlotte, Milwaukee and Sacramento, with Philadelphia right at the line.

Then there is my team. Boston’s trades the model hated worked out, on balance, and the veto would cost it $109M. Vetoing the same number of Boston’s trades at random would have cost $828M, so the model still picked out Boston’s weaker ones. They just weren’t weak enough. Utah loses the most from the veto. The costliest vetoes anywhere are the stuff of legend: Oklahoma City’s trade of Paul George that brought back Shai Gilgeous-Alexander (+$1884M), Toronto’s for Kyle Lowry (+$1032M), and Utah’s draft-night deal for Rudy Gobert (+$930M).

Are our heroes better than our villains? I can’t tell

Now the first question: do front offices differ from each other? Not who made the best trade or drafted the best player. Somebody always did. Whether knowing who made a decision tells you anything about how it turned out, and whether that difference is clear enough that it isn’t just the dice. Every number from here on is raw: a club’s or an executive’s own decisions, added up, with nothing taken out.

Two things to be good at. Process is making the decision my model liked on the day: for a trade, the Trade Lab’s grade; for a pick, the same flip as above, scored on draft night, which is how much of the draft board’s value a club left on the table by not taking the board’s favourite. Process gets no luck test. It’s my model’s own grade of each decision, so there’s no luck in it to take out; it just tells you who made the decisions the model liked. Results are what happened, and here luck is everything, so I keep four kinds apart: what a trade returned; what it returned beyond what trades graded like it usually return; out-picking the board (did the player you took produce more than the one the board would have taken?); and beating the forecast (did the player you took produce more than the board expected of him?). There are also two ways to sort the decisions: by franchise, and by the person running it on the day.6

Here’s how you tell, for results. Pretend every club is the same, so each decision is the league average plus its own bounce. Then rerun history 2,000 times, flipping and stretching the bounces at random, but the same way for every decision in a season, because a season’s luck is shared: a deep draft, a cap spike, a year everyone overpaid. Count how often the clubs end up as far apart as they really are. That’s the luck test.7 The size of a real difference is the spread left once you subtract what the dice produce on their own; below I call it the true spread. Then there’s the stockbroker problem: with 30 teams, one of them will always look special. The fix is a false discovery rate, which asks how often you’d be wrong calling a club this far out real, given how many you looked at.

Trades

Process first. 2,471 trade sides since 1999-00, and the average one graded −$12M on the day: the model is a grump.8 Houston’s trades graded best, about even at +$0.0M a side, then Boston’s, −$0.3M. By executive, Michael Winger is first.

Now results, and the part I was dreading. Boston’s trades returned +$24M a side, second only to Utah’s +$42M. One side swings by about $136M either way, and random histories spread the clubs at least this far 89% of the time. No true spread is left, by club or by executive. Utah clears the usual bar on its own; Boston doesn’t; and once you allow for having looked at all 30, calling even Utah real, you’d be wrong about one time in three.9 So I can’t tell Boston’s front office from a lucky coin: Rick Pitino at the start, then Chris Wallace, Danny Ainge for most of it, and Brad Stevens since. (Sigh.) I would like the record to show that the coin came up heads a lot.

The draft

Process first again. On draft night the average pick left $62M of the board’s value on the table. New Orleans, Dallas and Boston left the least; Washington, Cleveland and Sacramento the most. This is the one thing in the column that repeats itself: a franchise’s average in odd-year drafts predicts its average in even-year ones, at a correlation of 0.51.11 The caveat: “good process” here means agreeing with my model, which might be good drafting or might just be liking the same kind of player it does.

Now results, and the question I care about most: does anybody out-pick the board? It’s the flip from the top of this column: one pick at a time, the player’s value to the club over his career against the board’s pick’s. When the board would have taken the same player, the gap is zero. Denver’s Jokić pick counts for nothing: at 41, the board takes Jokić too. The board beats every club but Indiana and Miami, so the question is whether it beats some clubs by less than others, by more than luck would. It doesn’t: random histories spread the clubs at least this far 71% of the time. The executives do spread, by about $69M a pick, but random histories spread them that far 26% of the time. 12 executives’ picks did beat the board’s on average, led by Larry Bird and Jeff Peterson.

One club does stand out, even allowing for having looked at all 30, and it isn’t one I expected: New Orleans. Its picks still trailed the board’s, but by $6.5M a pick, against $61M for the average club, and that holds up at a false discovery rate of 4.5%. Indiana and Miami did better still, but on fewer picks and with more noise. The closest executive is Lawrence Frank, whose picks beat the board’s by $13M a pick (7%), and most of that is one of them: Shai.

The last question is different: did their players beat the forecast? Did the player a club took return more than even the board expected of him, over his whole career?12 This is where Jokić lives, and it isn’t evidence of choosing better than the model, since the board would have made the same pick. It’s mostly luck. The one skill that could hide in it is development: what a club does with a player after it takes him. It is also the closest thing in the column to a real difference. By executive, random histories spread them this far 1% of the time, inside the usual bar, with a true spread of $24M a pick. It doesn’t hold everywhere: count only executives with at least fifteen picks and it’s 19%; take out the ten biggest surprises and it’s 11%; leave out one draft class and it ranges from under 1% to 6%. The recent classes are mostly projection, too. Nobody is close to a name, either: allowing for having looked at 68 executives, the best false discovery rate is 47%. By franchise, Denver is first, and the clubs spread this far 13% of the time, with one club picked out at the other end: Washington, whose picks returned $40M a pick less than the board expected of them, at a false discovery rate of 6%.

Lawrence Frank, Larry Bird and Neil Olshey lead the executives. Lawrence Frank’s Clippers took Shai Gilgeous-Alexander 11th and traded him a year later for Paul George; the value stays with the club that was paid for him. Take out Nikola Jokić, Damian Lillard and Shai Gilgeous-Alexander and the executives spread this far 2% of the time; take out all ten and it’s 11%. Most of these executives did it once, and one draw can’t tell an eye for stars from standing in the right place.13 The exception is Sam Presti, with three of the ten: Kevin Durant, Russell Westbrook and James Harden, in three straight drafts. Last on the list is Jayson Tatum, the pick I was furious about in 2017. The board had him third, where he went. Nobody had him that good, least of all me.

Count the clubs and executives that clear the usual bar on their own. In trades, 10 of 164, about what luck alone would give (8). In the draft, 20 of 196, where luck would give about 10. Something in the draft is more than the dice. It might be real drafting and development; it might be the recent classes, which are mostly projection and could move a lot. I can’t tell which yet, and I’ll check again when they’ve played.

That is not the same as nobody being better. It means that a club would have to be enormously better to show up. To be picked out reliably from 30, a club would need to return about $52M a trade side more than the rest, or $173M a pick. A real edge of a few million a decision, which is a lot of money over a decade, is invisible in 27 seasons of trades and 20 drafts.14

Why it’s so hard to tell

Part of it is that the job pulls front offices together. They copy each other: the analytics department one club builds this year, the rest build next year, and a good idea doesn’t stay anyone’s edge for long. Part of it is that the worst of them don’t last. A truly terrible decision-maker gets fired before making enough decisions to show up in a test like this, so what I’m comparing is mostly the survivors.15 Neither means the job doesn’t matter. It means the people who keep it are closer to each other than to the people who lost it.

Every one of these trades is on the trades page, graded on the day and on how it turned out, and every one of these drafts is on the draft board. Go find your team’s favourite deal and see whether the model would have let it happen. It’s more fun than being right.
