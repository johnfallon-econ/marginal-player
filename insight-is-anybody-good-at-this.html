Is Anybody Actually Good at This?

As my grandfather always said, the biggest room in the house is room for improvement. That goes for front offices, too.

By Ben StupendousPosted Sep 27, 2026 · Updated Nov 26, 202619 min read

Let’s start by firing GarPax. Put Joe Dumars on 2 lines. Give the ball to Isiah and the NYK. There will be plenty of front offices to point to that looked clever over the last ~30 years. And many others that looked foolish, trying to reach the heights of Red Auerbach, who filled the Garden’s rafters.

So I constructed a draft and trade model, and asked it to make its picks and moves since 2007 and 1999-00 respectively. I then placed these alongside the actual decisions of each GM.

Could the model win? Yes

I’ll caveat that I know how these careers turned out, and so will any model I construct now. Therefore I put my model through the exact same gauntlet that each of the 30 franchises faced.1 I had to decide the form of the model given the knowledge of which players became which. The maximum benefit possible to each club is shown over.2

Assuming that the sequence of action leading up to each selection remained unchanged, the model instructed each club to select the Board’s highest ranked player remaining on each occasion from the 1st to final round of each draft.345 It would have altered 85% of the 1,133 selections made since 2007, resulting in 30 of 30 franchises being improved by an average of +$92M a pick. No franchise managed to out-perform the Board.

Across the 1st round, of those selections differing from the actual one, the Board’s player turned out better in 62%.6 Taking Jokić as an example, he is now estimated to be worth $2.1B to Denver (including the tail end of his career), and the board takes him at 18 different picks in 2014. Without these swaps, the gain per changed pick falls by roughly 33%. Removing the 10 players that drove the greatest increases, including Jokić, leaves the Board still winning.7

Donovan Mitchell, Paul George and Damian Lillard would all have fallen to another team. Its highest ranked player is Jokić and lowest Josh McRoberts. The Board selects him ahead of Mike Conley, Thaddeus Young and Al Horford at 34 different picks in 2007, swaps that cost about $2.1B. Nor would it fall for the ‘special’ qualities of Thon Maker (myself included!).

A parallel analysis was conducted for the period of trades. Any trade with a negative valuation by the model was disallowed for that franchise.8 A total of 50% trades out of 1,948 were prevented in this fashion, and every franchise but Utah saw a positive impact as a result (the trades it hated lost $47M a side on average, the ones it liked made $53M). Preventing the same proportion of a club’s trades at random led to lower returns for all 30, and significantly so for 27, with New York right at the line.9 Clearly, selecting the correct set of trades to prevent was key.

Had my franchise been subject to the process, the result would be that the Boston franchise avoids $845M. Furthermore, the model is able to identify specific poor trades made by the Celtics.10 (again!) As a consequence, only Utah fell behind after applying the process. However, several landmark trades were lost in turn, including Utah’s Rudy Gobert and Erick Green (+$908M) via 2013, and Luol Deng and Jackson Vroman (+$629M) from the Chicago franchise’s 2004. Finally, Washington would have failed to acquire 2004’s Devin Harris, Antawn Jamison and Christian Laettner (+$383M).

Heroes, Villains and Luck

As noted, each franchise will have enjoyed the benefit of at least one outstanding move or player acquisition. The question is whether any particular individual(s) did so sufficiently regularly to discount the role of chance.11121314

Trades

Of the 2,471 trade sides since 1999-00, the average graded +$0.1M on the day.15 Boston’s trades graded best (+$25M a side), then Houston’s (+$23M) and Oklahoma City’s (+$16M). By executive, it was Chris Grant.

Randomizing the assignment of trade sides to the relevant executive 20,000 times, the resulting group of franchises and individuals were at least as far apart in 28% and 96% of instances respectively. At the point of the trade, no executive stood out.

The realized mean return of the Boston franchise’s trades was +$35M, placing them 2nd of 30, behind San Antonio. The reshuffled clubs were at least that far apart 40% of the time.16

The draft

Using the Board’s projection, each selection had a mean value $113M lower than that of the Board’s favored player (New Orleans left $72M a pick, Phoenix $143M).20 Rolling the selections gave a comparable distribution of results in 8% of instances.2122 Only R.C. Buford had a materially different profile. His Spurs left $63M a pick, compared to a mean loss of $111M by the others.23 This tells us only the extent to which his selections aligned with my model. Whether he was an especially proficient drafter cannot be deduced from this.

However, I am primarily interested in whether any franchise was able to out-draft the Board. As discussed at the start of this piece, each player’s career value was compared to the Board’s alternative. The Board beat every club. Nobody’s record against the Board is better than chance: when the identity of the franchise making the picks was randomized, the clubs were at least this far apart in 83% of the shuffles.24

Nor does Jokić feature, as the Board would also have taken him at 41. Thus, there was no ‘head start’ for Denver. His career subsequently exceeded the Board’s expectation by a large margin, largely due to serendipity.25 Presumably, any club that excelled at player development would enjoy their picks exceeding the Board’s expectation more frequently. None did. Further randomization put the executives at least this far apart in 45% of cases and the clubs in 20%.26

Lawrence Frank’s fourth place is largely down to Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, whom his Clippers took 11th and a year later traded for Paul George.27 The table contains one pick from each of the 10 executives. So there’s no way a single pick will distinguish ability from randomness.28

Why it’s so hard to tell

Finding an edge is difficult. About ten executives would exceed the standard benchmark if their performance was purely due to luck in the trade market, and seven make that list. They would account for roughly seven if we consider luck in the draft, and four do.29 But on the whole, you lose value by trading away players, as they are likely to perform slightly less well elsewhere. (Perhaps moving house quickly is itself detrimental. Who knew?)30 So with the median executive responsible for 16 sets of trades and 12 selections, you require a large advantage to rise above your peers.31 And if there is an edge, it will be copied. Once one team builds an analytics department, the rest build one the next year. Similarly, it’s difficult to be markedly inferior, as you’ll be dismissed before your sample of decisions is sufficiently large.32 Of course, this shouldn’t imply that the role is unimportant, merely that those who continue to occupy it are clustered closely together in terms of their underlying ability.
