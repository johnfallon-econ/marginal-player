Our Numbers and Everyone Else’s

Marcus Smart was 224th in the league last season by the box score and 53rd by the model. Each kind of rating has a record at forecasting teams, and here it is.

By Ben StupendousPosted Sep 25, 2026 · Updated Nov 26, 202611 min read

All metrics published on this site derive from our model. Comparing their predictive ability to alternative metrics therefore provides an important context.

Marcus Smart played 2025-26 for the Lakers. Across the pool of 271 players1, he ranked 224th by VORP and 210th by Win Shares. Our model placed him 53rd, his largest separation from any of the box score-based metrics of the previous year.

Below are the positions of players by VORP and by the model. AJ Green was 259th by VORP and 56th by the model, and Herbert Jones was 250th and 82nd. Nic Claxton was 91st by VORP and 257th by the model, and Russell Westbrook was 149th and 261st.

Pascal Siakam took 31st and 184th from the model and Win Shares respectively. For Keldon Johnson, it was the opposite: 41st in the league by Win Shares and 191st by the model. Johnson’s 41st comes from Basketball-Reference’s Win Shares, based on the Dean Oliver Offensive and Defensive Ratings.

As noted above, Smart’s 224th relies solely on the box score. Starting from the box score and making adjustments using the scoreboard (i.e. the net effect of playing with each player on a team), Smart was ranked 53rd, and the three other ratings built the same way had him 58th, 70th and 103rd: Taylor Snarr’s EPM (from 2020), incorporating on-court tracking; DARKO (2019), updated daily; and BBall Index’s LEBRON. The scoreboard adjustment for Smart is RAPM. This seeks to remove the impact of the surrounding cast (on a face value basis, a reserve sharing court time with a superstar will appear to be one) and has its lineage from Joe Sill (2010), the initial public release by Dan Rosenbaum (2004), Wayne Winston and Jeff Sagarin (c. 2000). The latter two reportedly sold their product to the Mavericks for >$100kpa when the club was purchased by Mark Cuban. Our model represents another iteration of this class of metric, broadly adopted across the last ~10 years.

In 2025-26 DARKO had Jaylen Brown 30th and the model had him 128th. Taking the model’s aggregate metric mWAR (mPM is its league-wide rate), he was 85th. Note that as mWAR is restricted to a player’s actual games, they will register poorly in “street clothes”. Our model’s Trade Lab can similarly estimate the expected amount of court-time a given player like Brown will feature in future campaigns.2 In July, Boston sent him to the 76ers. As a local, I had hoped they would retain him. The model has consistently returned near-average values for Brown, spanning 3 consecutive years, and I did not find it comforting at the time.

I had DeMar DeRozan 97th by the model. DARKO had him 246th. Further, DARKO had Stephon Castle and Khris Middleton take 38th and 248th respectively, while my assessment was 152nd and 131st.

Across the 271 players, as a player’s steal rate increased, so too did DARKO, LEBRON and VORP’s league position vs. the model. The model gives steals less credit than any of these three ratings. Efficient scoring also moved a player toward VORP, and threes toward the model. In aggregate, the box score accounts for 12% of the discrepancy between the model’s ranking and VORP’s. Notably, Smart has a higher steal rate than 88% of these 271 players, yet remains 224th by VORP, principally due to his poor efficiency. As discussed previously, VORP is a sum of Myers’s rate, BPM. The former draws directly from the box score. VORP sat as BPM on the exams.3 On the first exam4 BPM missed teams’ scoring margins by 2.99 points a game. Players’ unadjusted +/− was similarly tracked (at least as early as 1974, by Harvey Pollack for the 76ers) missed by 3.52, and is the floor.

In 2021-22, on the exam Dunks & Threes designed, DARKO’s predictions resulted in a team-season error of 3.28 pts. For the model, this was 2.65. In 2020-21 DARKO was the closer one, 3.08 to 3.21.5 Two further metrics, PIPM (Jacob Goldstein, 2018) and FiveThirtyEight’s RAPTOR (2019), fall within this family. Over 2024-25, the respective errors were 3.36 and 3.41, such that DARKO was superior in only this one of the past 5 years. Last season, they were 4.06 and 3.86.

The model’s miss was smaller than DARKO’s in 16 of the 25 seasons from 2001-02, and its overall edge is one luck alone would produce 1% of the time. Similarly, in nine of the thirteen from 2013-14, and between 2001-02 and 2020-21 in 12 of 20. Since 2021-22 the model’s forecasts were closer in four of five; luck alone would produce that margin 6% of the time. Every rating’s team forecasts missed by more in the five seasons from 2021-22. DARKO’s error grew the most, surpassing even that of BPM. The reason for this is unclear, although DARKO makes the full historical record of each player publicly available at darko.app.6

Over the thirteen seasons from 2013-14 (when the league put SportVU tracking cameras in every arena), the model’s error was 2.79 pts. LEBRON, DARKO and John Hollinger’s PER (introduced in the early 2000s, scaling a player’s box score output by their team’s pace and share of minutes) were 2.90, 2.95 and 3.38 respectively.7 The model led each of the 3 components of the first exam, but its lead over BPM, RAPTOR, DARKO and RPM on margin only just cleared luck.

The model’s error in predicting the number of wins per season was 7.1. Given the sensitivity of this outcome to closely-contested fixtures, the model could not distinguish itself from LEBRON or RPM.

Luke Kennard was 64th by the model and 261st by LEBRON (RPM, whose numbers end in 2023-24, and RAPTOR, whose end in 2022-23, had no rank for him last season). LEBRON had Gary Payton II 53rd; the model had him 202nd. Across the thirteen seasons, it surpassed LEBRON in eight, and luck alone would produce its overall edge on the margin 7% of the time. In the postseason, LEBRON trailed the model on the preceding season’s figures and drew level on the current season’s.8

RPM was developed by Jeremias Engelmann and Steve Ilardi, ESPN, in 2014. ESPN subsequently added tracking data to the recipe from 2019-20.9

The model was able to outperform BPM and RAPTOR across each of the five seasons from 2001-02 through 2005-06. All remaining metrics (Win Shares, PER, plain RAPM and the initial box score) trailed throughout.

Over the thirteen seasons from 2013-14 there were 195 playoff series.10 The model put 59% on the team that won, and the team it rated higher won 75%, one fewer of the 195 than its own box-score starting guess picked right; the team with the better record the season before won 60%.11 Second Spectrum took tracking over from SportVU in 2017-18, and before it Kirk Goldsberry’s CourtVision, presented at the MIT Sloan conference in 2012, had put every shot on a map. In the playoffs a team’s five most-used players play 68% of its minutes (56% in the regular season) and its busiest player 80% of the game (66%). On the current season’s numbers the better record won 72% and the model 71%. The model and DARKO each had the eventual champion as its strongest team in six of the thirteen seasons from 2013-14; the team with the best record in the current season won five of the thirteen titles and the team with the best record the season before won three.
