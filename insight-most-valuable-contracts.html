The Most and Least Valuable Deals, in the Past and Today

Every negotiated NBA deal since 1999, and every one still running, valued as what the player was worth minus what he was paid.

By Ben StupendousPosted Oct 5, 2026 · Updated Nov 26, 20266 min read

In the summer of 2004 the Mavericks let Steve Nash leave and Phoenix signed him. Over the next two seasons he won two MVPs. His contract generated $813m in excess of Phoenix’s expenditure (adjusted to present day) and was therefore the most valuable since 1999-00.

I have done the same calculation for each negotiated deal since (including those still outstanding). 2/3rds generated a positive return, the median generating ~$8m.12345

Max deals feature prominently, for LeBron with the Heat and for Tim Duncan and Kobe Bryant during their peak. The top ten also has four extensions. The best of them is for Stephen Curry (2012, as he began to show ankle issues) who received ~$97m (present day) across four years and was worth ~$733m in excess of this.6

Grant Hill’s contract in Orlando is among the ten worst, nine of which started before 2004. However, in 4 of the years covered by these contracts Hill appeared in <50% of the team’s games and sat out the fifth.7 Vin Baker fared the worst of all, losing ~$184m relative to the $221m paid to him. None of the ten lost more than the team paid out.

Trae Young’s contract is the worst of the active contracts, with ~$177m owed by Washington across four years and the model projecting ~$94m ‘underwater’. Next is Jaylen Brown, whose ~$63m underwater liability spans his remaining three years, taken on by Philly in July (I’d have liked to see Boston retain him).10

Why are the most so much larger?

Since 1999-00, the spread of values between the most and least valuable contracts is $813m to −$184m. You can view the full set of active contracts on our Value Board by selecting your team.1112

Values are aggregated across each player’s time on our Players board (from 1999-00 to 2025-26), using data from our Contract Ledger. For active contracts, our Value Board is used. All figures are in present-day dollars (as at the date of this compilation). Wins are on this model’s own scale and dollars at the open-market price of a win. See here for details.

  1. Across the period’s 2,419 contracts, median of the applicable sample. ↩
  2. This quantity corresponds to the economic concept of ‘surplus’. Specifically, it is the mWAR for the relevant season multiplied by the open-market value of a win, less his salary; the method has the price. ↩
  3. Each season’s surplus as a share of that season’s cap, times the 2025-26 cap of $155M. The cap was $34M in 1999-00. ↩
  4. The Contract Ledger treats extensions as being made in addition to the base contract. While appropriate for trade valuation, it is not ideal here. Split at the extension, the new money starts the season after it was signed; a veteran extension signed two years early is credited a year too soon. ↩
  5. An option will typically be exercised if the player is worth less than the amount payable. Hence a club will retain the period where he is worth < the option and forgo the period where he is worth > the option. Similarly, if a club holds an option they will decline to re-sign a player if he is worth less than the option, and re-sign him otherwise. Uncertainty around the projection is taken into account. The initial 3 seasons of a player are excluded, as the likelihood of them being subject to the rookie contract is high. Subsequent contracts are assumed to take the form of 4-year deals until the player reaches 40. At the end of each, the probability of the player remaining with his club is inferred from the behavior of comparable players, and the amount payable is either the Max or the re-signing value for comparables. This approach is also applied by the Trade Lab and Value Board when considering player re-signings. ↩
  6. As noted above, a club extending a player will be aware of his true value, and can secure his services ahead of any revelation of the open-market price. On a per-season basis, no player exceeded Stephen Curry’s. ↩
  7. Contracts tended to span a greater number of years, increasing the potential period of ‘loss’ if a deal went badly. 26 of the 50 least valuable contracts commenced prior to 2005, during which time 19% of all contracts were signed. Contracts lasted 3.4 and 2.7 years on average in the pre-2005 and post-2011 periods respectively. ↩
  8. $203M over five seasons, on $46M in all. ↩
  9. $472M in all, with two seasons left on his current deal. ↩

  12. There is a natural upper bound to how much a poor contract can hurt a club, approximately limited to the payment made. This is increased marginally if the player is worse than a minimum contract player. Conversely, there is no limit to the benefit a contract can deliver given that the club’s exposure is bounded while the player’s output is not. This gives rise to a convex function, implying that clubs should enter into a greater proportion of such contracts than their raw negative tail would indicate, and go ‘all-in’ in the Free Agency market. ↩
