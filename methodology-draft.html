Projecting a prospect

The interesting question about any draft pick is usually not “what will his career look like?”. More commonly, we want to know how uncertain it is, and what its worst case looks like. The outputs are thus distributions.

What is predicted

For each draft year, there are three targets, all given in mWAR in a fully-available player: (i) his mPM in that year less replacement level, scaled by β̂ and multiplied by his average minutes-share over a normal season; (ii) the average of his rates over the years (if any) that he has played to date, weighted by those years’ minutes and evaluated at the corresponding minutes-weighted role; and (iii) his highest single-season value over the first 7 years. All targets apply only in years where a player has played ≥100 minutes.

  • Rookie year: his realized output in season 1.
  • Four-year mean: his average over the first 4 years, if he played.
  • Peak: his highest single season over the first 7 years, if he played.

As noted, for recent draft years these span fewer years.

Note that the “highest” target has an inherent upwards bias: any constant-player will record some seasons higher than his overall mean. This is simply a consequence of taking the max over multiple noisy observations. So the target should be read as “how high did he ever get?” rather than “how good was he at his best?”, and the two differ when seasons are measured with error.

We similarly choose to anchor the other two horizons to the draft, rather than taking a player’s career average. The latter would clearly depend on when he entered the league, simply as a function of how many years would lie ahead of him.

Censoring, and why busts must count

If the training set consisted solely of successful picks, the model would learn that all picks go on to play in the NBA. As a result, all subsequent predictions would assume this, leaving the tail of the fan entirely unpopulated.

Instead, we adopt a hurdle approach with two separate prediction problems. First, the model must learn the likelihood that a prospect goes on to play at least one NBA game. This is learned from all available data points from prior draft years, i.e. drafted and undrafted prospects for whom we know if they played or not (having played ≥1 game or completed their initial 4-year period without doing so). Second, conditional on a player having played, we predict his degree of availability and quality. Crucially, this ensures that the model is not tasked with predicting the value of a player who simply didn’t play.

These two pieces can then be combined when computing a prospect’s value. Specifically, the value of each prospect is a mixture of a lower bound (set to be less than the worst player who plays) and his expected played-seasons’ availabilities and qualities, weighted by his likelihood of playing. This has the desirable effect that prospects with low probabilities of ever sticking will rank significantly below a passable rotational contributor, while still ensuring a non-trivial mass of “never plays” is represented in the fan.

Finally, for each prospect, any future seasons are omitted from the target, i.e. treated as unobserved rather than assumed to be 0.

What the board ranks on: value to the drafting club

While the preceding discussion gives an account of how we estimate the quality and availability of a player, these do not directly correspond to the asset acquired by the drafting team. A team will retain a prospect for the duration of his rookie deal (subject to trading or release), and may choose to re-sign him at the end of this and subsequent contracts. If he reaches superstar status, they will be required to pay him the maximum, substantially less than his contribution. Accordingly, the board displays the expected cumulative value of each prospect to the drafting team, expressed in the draft-year’s salary terms, broken down by individual season.

The career, season by season

The model generates a projection for each of the first 20 years of a player’s career. Seasons 1-7 are therefore predicted using separate quality and availability models. There are insufficient players with sufficiently extended careers to warrant additional models (564 players have a quality label in season 8, 71 in season 16 and 7 in season 20), so seasons 8-20 are predicted by a single gradient-boosted model per quantile, fit on every player-season from 8 on with the year as an additional covariate to ensure continuity in the forecast.

Conformally widened and re-scaled

All fans displayed on the board are first subjected to the conformal widening procedure discussed in “Are the intervals honest?”. The individual season forecasts used to calculate the total club value are then adjusted twice, based on the model’s own forecast and learned separately from each prior draft year as described. Notably, neither adjustment incorporates the player’s draft slot, nor is the draft slot fed into the base-quality and availability predictions. However, the model used to determine the club value clearly accounts for the slot, since teams differ in the extent to which they retain, re-sign and pay the maximum to high vs low picks, holding output fixed. Consequently, the expected length of the rookie deal, probability of re-signing, likelihood of a max-contract and the proportion of the cap paid at each deal are all predicted as a function of the slot. Nevertheless, each prospect is displayed and compared as though he had been selected at a common evaluation slot (the #1 pick, unless overridden by the user under “picking at” on the board). Thus the slot has no bearing on how any particular prospect is valued relative to others, and hence also to his position in the draft. The features of the evaluation slot’s corresponding rookie-scale are likewise applied.

Spread first. First, we observe that the season-level distributions remain somewhat under-dispersed. Ideally, a given season’s actual value should fall uniformly within its corresponding forecast. In practice, however, too few seasons land in the middle and too many in the tails, busts above all. In particular, for lottery picks in seasons 3-6, 10.8% fell below the 5th percentile of their forecast and 17.0% landed in its 50th-75th band, compared with the expected 5% and 25%. We address this by re-mapping the levels of each forecasted distribution to match the empirical frequencies of realized values for similarly-forecasted player-seasons (with the mapping differing by year). On classes the mapping had not seen, these shares become 8.9% and 22.4%. This partially alleviates the issue, although there remains limited separation between mid-to-high lottery picks and lower ones.

Then level, in dollars. Second, each year’s conformally-widened value is transformed to match the observed level of similarly-valued player-seasons, with the mapping constrained to be monotone and including a $0 term for the never-played population, fit on seasons 1–16. Each season’s value is then rescaled by this factor to maintain the ranking of player-seasons. For the most recent draft, the net effect is to decrease the lower end and increase the upper end of the distribution, mapping a forecast of ~33%/10%/1% of the cap to 45%/14%/0.7% respectively. Note that the model’s raw (pre-adjustment) career path for each player is shown on the accompanying graph.

Retained, re-signed and paid the max

  • Rookie deal. During a player’s rookie-scale period, the team captures 100% of his output. A player’s rookie deal will begin at the start of his first contracted season, potentially some time following the draft if he has spent previous seasons overseas. Any preceding seasons are included in the calculation and assigned the model’s prediction for those years. The period spans 4 years on the first-round scale, and the length of the deal he signs in the second round. Since the board has no insight into the specific terms of each prospect’s deal, it instead averages over the distribution of lengths known to be available to him at the point of his selection, inferred from the prior years’ cohorts. These depend on whether he signs in his draft year, his round of selection, and whether he is an international recruit (notably, only 31% of 2nd round internationals did so, compared to 99% of domestic 1st-rounders), and the length of a second-round deal (59% sign for one or two seasons). As above, the former applies to the evaluation slot. Subsequently, the team retains him for each successive year with a probability predicted from all players drafted since 1999, dependent on his win-total in the preceding year, the year of the deal, his round and (logged) draft slot and (logged) slot√wins. Notably, 88% of lottery picks remained in their deal until its 4th year and 83% of 2nd-round picks for 3 years. The model’s out-of-sample performance (measured by log-loss) is 0.186 against a base rate’s 0.267; without the round and slot, 0.201.
  • Re-signings. When a player’s current deal expires, he will stay with his team subject to the model’s prediction, derived from the historical record of expiring deals. After the initial period, this is conditioned on the player’s team retaining him for the duration of the prior deal, his win-total at the end of that deal, age, (logged) slot and √wins/slot. Restricted free agency keeps 94% of players worth 5-10 mWAR; log loss 0.561 against a base rate’s 0.678, and 0.564 without the slot term. As with the rookie-scale, the slot exerts the strongest influence. Predicting just 1/(slot) yields marginally improved log-loss (0.559), but keeps nearly every bust taken first, and the board requires every prospect to be valued at one slot. Consistent with this, the model correctly identifies that 60% of the first 2 picks generated ≤1mWAR in the final year of their deal stayed with their team, vs 41% of 2nd-round picks, and predicts 59%/38% out of sample. Finally, for each subsequent period, the model is trained on the records of expiring deals recorded on the database, treating any “extension” as equivalent to the player staying and the deal concluding. Log-loss of 0.595 vs baseline of 0.655.
  • Traded players. If a team elects to trade a player, the burden of paying for him transfers to the recipient, such that only a player departing his team (via free agency, dismissal or retirement) results in a complete loss. The latter two cases are subsumed under a player failing to “stay”. Given the season-level models, there is no obvious distinction to be made between a player continuing to perform at a replacement level and one leaving the game entirely, so the model must make this judgement. Note that the probability of an outcome falling at the never-played level will vary depending on the round associated with the evaluation slot. In particular, as noted above, 1st-round picks will sign the scale and continue to be retained (at the rate of a player recording 0 wins) throughout, and then face the same decision at the conclusion of their deal as any other. Assuming the player is assessed at his own slot, first-round picks of 2007–2016 were predicted held in rookie-deal seasons 2-4 89% of the time against 88% realized (Brier score 0.099), and kept past the deal 46% against 49%. Similarly, for 2nd-round picks, the majority of floor outcomes correspond to the player failing to sign a deal or being released shortly thereafter, and hence not being retained.
  • Subsequent periods. Upon re-signing, a player’s value will be diminished by the sum he commands. If this is the maximum, it is determined by the CBA: 25% of the cap for the second deal, 30% after 7 years of service and 35% after 10. The probability that a given re-signing is a max will be predicted as a function of his mWAR and age at the time, and whether the prior deal was a rookie-scale, incorporating the (logged) slot at the commencement of his initial contract (conditioned on him signing it) as an additional feature, since the maximum will occur as an outlier at the extreme of the pay-scale, and is much more likely to be offered to a younger player. Elsewhere, the player will command the same percentage of the cap as his peers, predicted from the relevant subset of re-signings. Importantly, because a team will generally pay below-market rates to re-sign its own players, there will be residual value accruing to them even in the absence of a max deal. Finally, as a player’s contract fixes his income for its duration, the team will incur a shortfall for each year that he generates less than his salary, which the board reflects.
  • Known at the time of the draft. The re-signing, scale-length, max-deal and salary models applied to each draft year are therefore re-fitted using only contract outcomes from before that draft. Players’ wins will of course be informed by the revised level estimates discussed above.

There are 40 outcome draws per prospect. At each point along the simulated career, the probability weights apply: stars will almost certainly be retained, re-signed to max and delivered that; strong players retained below max; marginal ones frequently lost or depart free, but increasingly unlikely as you go up the evaluation slot, such that the fits will see these players stay and earn > their value. The expected output is either the simple mean, or adjusted by the user’s “risk” setting. These values drive the draft-board rankings of all available prospects at the given slot, whose relative ordering can change as the slot is cycled. Draft “surplus” is the difference to the corresponding rookie-scale offer, i.e. the amount a team would have to spend to acquire any given prospect at that slot. For slots after pick #30, we assume a 4-year minimum contract.

How good is it?

We compare to the actual output of each class of draftees, 2007–2016, in terms of their drafting club’s returns. Specifically: wins generated (at the draft-night open-market rate) whilst the player(s) were on their original or a traded-for contract under their rookie-scale, and (subsequently, provided they remained under that contract) their value net of contract. We only consider this until the final season for which a given class has recorded output, i.e. seasons 10-19. As noted, the focus is on the fits (i.e. the player(s) filling the draft slot in question), so the value attributed to each player is that of the slot they were selected in. Below we similarly compare the relative merit of all prospects at each evaluation slot. Naturally we restrict this comparison to players who have appeared on an NBA roster, treating those who never earned a contract as “not retained” for both the final two columns.

The frequency with which top-5 picks were retained beyond their rookie-scale is lower than in reality (73% vs 74%), and their projected (per-player retained) value after that point exceeds their actual output ($61.3M vs $50.7M). There is some cancellation, leading to $44.6M vs $37.5M over all top-5 picks. Elsewhere in the lottery and 2nd round, retention rates are broadly in line with the actuals (54% and 51%; 24% and 26%), although subsequent value is projected above them ($30.9M vs $25.1M; $6.2M vs $4.6M). However, the group 15–30 were retained at a rate 34% vs the actual 41%. All actuals are in this website’s native metrics, so we can only assess whether our model is internally consistent with its own basis.

Scouting reports, and their authorship

As set out, nearly all of the features fed to the model are objective measures. With one exception: every prospect who has one has a scouting rubric associated with them, displayed across the 10 categories (1-10) along their draft board entry.

It should be clear that these are not this website’s view of those prospects. The base data comes from NBADraft.net’s existing pre-draft pages, comprising an explicit list of attributes and a narrative section (“Strengths”, “Weaknesses”) contributed by their in-house scouts. We pass the latter through an LLM to derive the 10 scores, covering: shooting, defensive ability, athletic capacity, basketball IQ, effort, ability to generate own shot, upside, bust risk, and two flags for injury and character concerns. So in effect the LLM is simply converting another party’s qualitative assessment into quantitative form.

The two flagged dimensions deserve their own sentence. We single out the final 2 of these 10. As with the remainder, a score of 10 indicates the maximum level of issue. We instruct the model to return 1 if the relevant topic is not mentioned in the scout’s comments, and 5 for all others absent specific mention. As can be seen, the behavioral issue similarly clusters towards the low end of the scale. Of the 1,191 reports reviewed, 1,129 have the injury flag at 1 and 1,136 the character flag at 1 or 2. It is important to disclose the provenance of these features to the reader, and to flag their unusual nature.

Again, the sample is non-random. 1,191 of the prospects reviewed have an accompanying NBADraft.net page, and the remainder are marked “no scouting report on file”. Presumably the former are the players their site deems most newsworthy to their readership, i.e. largely concentrated at the head of each class. The model thus has richest guidance here, and must rely purely on the objective metrics for those left undrafted.

NBADraft.net provides two separate attribute lists for “Guard” and “Wing” vs “Center” and “Power Forward”, the former having “Ball Handling” and “Passing” and the latter “Post Skills” and “Rebounding”. We consume both, retaining the additional features for the big-men (404). NBADraft.net pages are retrieved by concatenating the player’s name with ‘-’ delimiters, allowing us to capture entries for e.g. Karl-Anthony Towns and Shai Gilgeous-Alexander.

Timing. Finally, we note that the pages consumed are those currently live on NBADraft.net. Our process assumes that no changes have been made to them since the preceding draft. If this is untrue, some degree of look-ahead will creep in. Nonetheless, as long as we can regard these as pre-draft, they constitute valid inputs.

Model specification

To recover the τ-quantile, we solve for parameters minimizing:

max(τ(y−ŷ), (τ−1)(y−ŷ))

This has an expected minimum at the desired quantile. By estimating each of the {0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.90, 0.95, 0.99} in turn, we avoid imposing any shape (e.g. normality) on the predictive distribution. Indeed, the 4-year average is heavily right-skewed, with a median of 0.9 mWAR and 95th %ile of 7.3, and 6% of the players who have one sit below replacement.

The board publishes quantiles estimated from a Gradient Boosted Machine (LightGBM), incorporating the player’s league (as a factor). We additionally estimate a Neural Network with an embedded representation of the player’s league, and compare its performance; given the modest N, we report the average prediction across 5 random initializations.

Features include (competition-normalized) college and overseas stats, Combine data, public large-sample draft boards, HS recruiting status and pre-draft All-Star events, the scouting-report features above and a time-period effect. Notably, draft pick is not included. We hold it out so that the model can be compared with the market fairly, as below.

The model(s) are trained on 4,741 prospects from the 1995 to 2026 cohorts, projecting onto ~301 transformed variables. Note that the 210 (i.e. 2026) cohort have not yet completed a single NBA season, and hence their output is purely prospective. Similarly, 2,567 have failed to register a season in their opening 4 years. We therefore train the quality models only on the 1,496 subset; the likelihood of playing is learned from every prospect whose outcome is known.

Testing the model(s)

We adopt an expanding window of draft classes, training on the earliest and testing on successively later. The NN halts early using an inner split of the most recent 2 training classes. The GBM is trained for a set number of 400 iterations, without an internal hold-out. Crucially, the test cohort is not seen at any stage. All results below reflect this.

We compute the rank-correlation between the predicted median and actual on-court quality of each draftee (mWAR), and compare this to the ranking implied by their draft position. The shares of outcomes falling below the predicted median lie in the range 0.475-0.536 vs the nominal 0.50, implying an approximately unbiased forecast at all horizons. However, there is material under-coverage at each extreme. Approximately 14% of players fall below the 10th and 17% above the 90th quantile (both targeted at 10%), and for peak the miss is larger at the top (17% above the 90th, 13% below the 10th).

Rather than force the 95th and 99th %iles via extrapolation from the 90th, we fit these directly. The former is clearly where the vast majority of a draftee’s realized value will lie. 4% of peak observations exceed the 99th %ile, against a target of 1%.

Accordingly, each of the fans displayed by the board (including the 4-year and peak quality estimates, whether a player is available, their O/D splits and individual-year outputs) has been expanded conformally. That is, we scale the distance of each level from the median by a constant determined using only the preceding classes’ unadjusted fan(s) and actual observation(s), sufficient to ensure the appropriate coverage. This constant is bounded to 0.5-8, and importantly each class’ expansion was fitted excluding their own data. Where a given class had <150 prior observations, we show the unexpanded fan. The subsequent year-by-year draftee valuations are re-calibrated consistent with the procedure in the previous section.

Limitations

  • Recent classes are scored on part of the window. Note that, given we truncate each horizon to the number of seasons each draftee has played to date, the most recently-drafted cohort are assessed over a shorter period than indicated. E.g. only 761 of the 909 class have completed 4 full seasons, and 618 of the 916 have played 7. Thus, the validation exercise is most informative with respect to the furthest-out classes.
  • Pre-draft data is thinner internationally. The volume of competition-adjusted overseas data is reduced, although this is mitigated.
  • A distribution is only as good as the record it was fit to. Finally, any truly novel draftee will necessarily be the hardest case for the model to predict.
  • Draft order is a weak benchmark. And of course we are only comparing to the order in which players were picked, so beating it shows the model adds information, not that it is close to the achievable frontier.
  • The later seasons rest on older classes. Also, we can only publish projections up to season 20, and these apply to the draftee pool only to the extent that classes up to 2006 have been seen. On that basis, the predicted median mWAR for draftees over seasons 17-19 was on average 3.7 mWAR below their actuals, on 4–15 players a season.
